Nicholas Winton and the 669 Children of 1939

Strange History · November 17, 2026 · By Poss Jonah

In December 1938, a 29-year-old London stockbroker was about to go on a ski holiday. Instead, a friend asked him to come to Prague. What he saw there changed his life, and saved hundreds of others. His name was Nicholas Winton. For nearly fifty years, almost nobody knew what he had done.

Who he was

Nicholas Winton was born on May 19, 1909, in Hampstead, London. His parents, of German-Jewish ancestry, had the family name Wertheim; the family later changed it to Winton in 1938. He went to Stowe School, trained in banking in London, Hamburg, Berlin and Paris, and became a stockbroker at the London Stock Exchange. He was also a competitive fencer, and he followed the news from Germany with growing alarm.

Why the rescue was urgent

On September 30, 1938, the Munich Agreement allowed Nazi Germany to take the Sudetenland, the border region of Czechoslovakia. Refugees, including Jewish families and political opponents of the Nazis, poured into what was left of the country. In November 1938, the violence of Kristallnacht in Germany and Austria showed how dangerous things had become for Jewish communities. Britain agreed that month to take in unaccompanied refugee children from Germany and Austria, the program known as the Kindertransport. But there was no organized program yet for children in Czechoslovakia.

Prague

In December 1938, Winton's friend Martin Blake, who was working with the British Committee for Refugees from Czechoslovakia, asked him to come to Prague. There Winton met Doreen Warriner, a young academic who ran the committee's Prague office, and he visited crowded refugee camps. He decided the children of Czechoslovakia needed a rescue of their own. Parents began lining up at his hotel to register their children.

Making it happen

Back in London, Winton did the paperwork side. He worked at the stock exchange by day and on the rescue in the evenings. He had to find a British foster family for every child, get entry permits, and raise the £50 guarantee the British government required for each child. Foster families were found partly by circulating the children's photographs.

In Prague, an English schoolmaster named Trevor Chadwick ran the departures, and others, including Warriner, Blake and Czech helpers, made the operation possible. Winton always insisted that others deserved credit and that he was not the only one. The first group left by plane on March 14, 1939, the day before German troops occupied Prague. After that, children traveled by train across Germany to the Dutch coast and by ship to Britain, where foster families met them at the station. There were eight transports between March and August 1939, and the last successful train left Prague on August 2, 1939.

The train that never left

A ninth transport, carrying about 250 children, was due to leave on September 1, 1939. That day Germany invaded Poland, the borders closed, and the train never departed. Most of those children did not survive the war. Winton carried that loss for the rest of his life.

A scrapbook in the attic

After the war, Winton rarely spoke about the rescue. He married Grete Gjelstrup and raised a family. In the late 1980s, his wife came across a scrapbook with lists of names, photographs and letters. It reached Holocaust historian Elisabeth Maxwell, and newspapers picked up the story in 1988. That year, the BBC program That's Life!, presented by Esther Rantzen, invited Winton into the studio audience. Without warning him, the host asked whether anyone present owed their life to him. The people around him stood up. They were some of the children he had helped, now adults.

The accepted total is 669 children, based on the scrapbook and later research, though the U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum notes that the exact number is not certain. Most were Jewish. Many of them went on to have children and grandchildren of their own, and their descendants today number in the thousands.

Honors and legacy

Winton was made an honorary citizen of Prague in 1991, was knighted by Queen Elizabeth II in 2003, and in 2014, at age 105, received the Czech Republic's highest honor, the Order of the White Lion. He died on July 1, 2015, at age 106. A statue of him stands at Prague's main railway station. His life was the subject of the 2023 film One Life, based on a biography by his daughter, Barbara Winton.

Asked about being called a hero, Winton pushed back. He said he was never in danger himself. But his story shows that ordinary people, using ordinary skills like paperwork, fundraising and persistence, can do extraordinary good.
