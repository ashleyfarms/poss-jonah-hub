Seabiscuit vs. War Admiral: The Family Feud of 1938

Strange History · November 17, 2026 · By Poss Jonah

On November 1, 1938, two racehorses met at Pimlico in Baltimore in a race that seemed to stop the country. One was War Admiral, the 1937 Triple Crown winner. The other was Seabiscuit, a small, knobby-kneed horse that had lost far more races than he won early on. About 40 million people are estimated to have listened on the radio. What many listeners did not know was that the two were family: both traced back to the great champion Man o' War.

The family tree

Man o' War was a big red (chestnut) champion of 1919 and 1920. War Admiral was his son, a brown horse much smaller than his father. Seabiscuit's sire, Hard Tack, was another son of Man o' War, which made Seabiscuit the champion's grandson, and War Admiral's half-nephew. One of the most famous rivalries in American sports was, in a sense, a family affair.

War Admiral, the champion

War Admiral won the 1937 Kentucky Derby, a race his father never ran. He won the Preakness, and then the Belmont Stakes, even though he stumbled at the start and cut his own hoof. He won by three lengths anyway and became the fourth Triple Crown winner in history. He won all eight of his races in 1937, and over his career he won 21 of 26 starts.

Seabiscuit, the underdog

Seabiscuit's early record was rough. At age two, in 1935, he ran 35 races and won only five. His first trainer, the famous "Sunny Jim" Fitzsimmons, reportedly considered him lazy. In August 1936, businessman Charles Howard bought him for about $8,000, and trainer Tom Smith, a quiet horseman, figured out how to bring him along. Seabiscuit started winning big races, especially in California, and became a favorite of ordinary fans during the Great Depression. His regular jockey, Red Pollard, was blind in one eye and kept it hidden.

The match race

Owners had argued for months about a showdown. When it finally happened at Pimlico, War Admiral was the favorite. Tom Smith had trained Seabiscuit to break fast at the starting bell, and Seabiscuit took the early lead. With Red Pollard out injured (he had badly broken his leg in June 1938), George Woolf rode Seabiscuit. War Admiral drew even down the backstretch, but Seabiscuit pulled away and won by four lengths. The Daily Racing Form reported a track record of 1:56 3/5 for the mile and three-sixteenths. Seabiscuit was named 1938 Horse of the Year.

Injury and comeback

Both horse and jockey were soon hurt. Seabiscuit ruptured a ligament in his leg, and sources differ on the exact date. Many people thought both careers were over. Instead, horse and rider recovered together. On March 2, 1940, with Pollard back in the saddle, Seabiscuit won the Santa Anita Handicap in front of about 78,000 people, beating Kayak II by a length and a half. He retired as the all-time leading money winner with $437,730. Over 89 starts he won 33 races.

Why the country cared

The match race happened in the middle of the Great Depression, when millions of Americans were out of work or just getting by. Seabiscuit had been cheap, small, overlooked and written off, and plenty of listeners saw something of themselves in him. His owner, Charles Howard, was a car dealer who had started out repairing bicycles, and his trainer, Tom Smith, was a quiet former mustang breaker. Their story was told again in Laura Hillenbrand's best-selling 2001 book Seabiscuit: An American Legend and in the 2003 movie Seabiscuit, which introduced the horse to a new generation.

Where they rest

Seabiscuit died in 1947 at age 13 and rests at Ridgewood Ranch in Willits, California; the exact spot is a family secret. War Admiral died in 1959 at age 25 and was buried beside his father, Man o' War. Their graves are now at the Kentucky Horse Park in Lexington. Both horses are in the National Museum of Racing Hall of Fame.
