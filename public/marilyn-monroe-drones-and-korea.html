Marilyn Monroe, Drone Builder and Troop Entertainer: Two Wartime Stories

Strange History · November 17, 2026 · By Poss Jonah

Most people know Marilyn Monroe from movie posters. Fewer know that two of the most important moments of her life were tied to the U.S. military. In World War II, a teenage factory worker named Norma Jeane was discovered on an aircraft assembly line. And in 1954, newly married and at the height of her fame, she took a detour from her honeymoon to perform for American troops in Korea.

Part 1: The drone factory

During World War II, young Norma Jeane Dougherty was married to her first husband, James Dougherty, who was serving in the merchant marine. She moved in with his parents and took a job at the Radioplane Company, a war plant in Van Nuys in the Los Angeles area. Accounts describe ten-hour days for about $20 a week.

Radioplane made something that sounds modern: small, radio-controlled target aircraft. Anti-aircraft gunners needed realistic moving targets to practice on, and these little planes, flown from the ground by radio, were cheaper and more realistic than towing a cloth target behind a manned airplane. The company had been started by Reginald Denny, a British-born Hollywood actor who had flown in World War I and became fascinated with radio-control model airplanes in the 1930s. Radioplane's OQ-2 went into Army service in 1941, and the faster OQ-3 became the most-used target aircraft in the United States during the war. By the end of the war the company and a production partner had built nearly fifteen thousand drones.

The photographer

In late 1944, an Army photographer named David Conover came to the factory. He worked for the Army Air Forces' First Motion Picture Unit, whose commanding officer was Captain Ronald Reagan, and he had been sent to take morale-boosting pictures of women doing war work. He photographed Norma Jeane working on the line. He later wrote that he was struck by her right away. His photographs of her ran in Yank, the Army Weekly in 1945. Encouraged by Conover, she quit the factory in January 1945 and began modeling. Within about a year and a half she had a studio contract and a new name: Marilyn Monroe. She later credited Conover with discovering her.

Part 2: The honeymoon detour

Jump ahead to 1954. Marilyn Monroe married baseball legend Joe DiMaggio at San Francisco City Hall on January 14, 1954. About two weeks later, they flew to Japan, combining a honeymoon with a business trip of his. While she was there, the military asked whether she would entertain U.S. troops in Korea. The Korean War had ended in an armistice the previous July, but tens of thousands of American servicemen were still stationed there through the winter.

She said yes. DiMaggio stayed in Japan. Her Department of Defense noncombatant identity card for the tour, issued February 8, 1954, is made out to "DiMaggio, Norma Jeane."

Four days, ten shows

Over four days in February 1954 she performed at about ten military camps across South Korea, traveling between them by helicopter, airplane and jeep. The weather was bitterly cold. Photos from the tour show soldiers and Marines bundled in parkas while she performed in a thin cocktail dress. A U.S. Marine Corps photo caption from February 16, 1954, describes her singing for an estimated 13,000 men of the First Marine Division at one show. Estimates of the total audience vary: some accounts say more than 60,000 Marines, and an Army estimate put the total near 100,000 servicemen.

Many of the photographs taken that week were made by military photographers, which is why they are part of the public record today.

Why these stories matter

Both stories show a side of Monroe that her movie image tends to hide: a young woman doing war work for low pay, and a star who took time from her honeymoon to stand on a freezing stage for soldiers far from home. They are also a reminder that big careers often start with small, ordinary moments, like an Army photographer walking onto a factory floor.
