The Sailing Stones of Death Valley: How Rocks Move on Their Own

Strange Science · September 29, 2026

In Death Valley National Park there is a dry lake bed called Racetrack Playa. The ground is flat and cracked like a puzzle, and scattered across it are rocks, some weighing hundreds of pounds, each with a long trail behind it in the mud. It looks like someone dragged them.

For decades, nobody knew who or what did it. The park service says the rocks range up to about 700 pounds (320 kg). People have wondered about wind, water, and ice since the 1940s. Nobody had ever seen one move.

Putting GPS in the rocks

Scientists finally decided to catch it in the act. Richard Norris, a scientist at Scripps Institution of Oceanography, and his cousin Jim Norris placed GPS units inside some rocks and set up weather stations and cameras.

In December 2013, they arrived to find the playa covered by a shallow pond about three inches deep. Cold nights froze it into ice that was only a few millimeters thick, thin as a windowpane.

What actually happened

As the sun came up and the ice began to melt, it broke into floating panels. A light breeze, around 4 to 5 meters per second, pushed the panels across the water. As the sheets drifted, they shoved the rocks along the soft mud underneath, leaving the trails.

On December 20, 2013, more than sixty rocks moved. They traveled at a few meters per minute. The researchers published their findings in the journal PLOS ONE in 2014. Richard Norris later called it "complete dumb luck" that they were there when the conditions lined up (The New York Times, 2014).

No mystery left, but don't try it yourself

The explanation is not aliens or magnets. It is a rare combination of a thin pond, thin ice, sun, and a gentle wind. That is also why the movement is rare and rarely witnessed.

The National Park Service asks visitors not to move the rocks or drive on the playa, because tracks and disturbances damage a fragile, unusual landscape.

Where the stones come from

Racetrack Playa is a dry lake bed high in a remote valley of Death Valley National Park. Most of its stones are dark dolomite that breaks off a steep rocky slope, about 850 feet (260 meters) high, at the south end of the playa. Others are syenite and other igneous rock from nearby slopes. Once a stone tumbles onto the flat clay, it sits there until the right conditions come along, and that can be years.

Reading the tracks

The trails tell a lot. Stones with rough bottoms tend to carve straight, scratched tracks, while smooth-bottomed stones wander. Stones sometimes flip and leave a different track. Two stones that start side by side can travel parallel for a while, then one turns sharply left, right, or even back the way it came. Some trails are hundreds of feet long. During the December 2013 event, some rocks moved up to about 224 meters (245 yards). A big dolomite block the researchers nicknamed "Karen," estimated at about 700 pounds, did not move at all while they watched, even though it sits at the end of a long old track.

A century of guesses

The first recorded account comes from 1915, when a prospector from Nevada named Joseph Crook visited the playa. Geologists mapped the area in the following decades, Life magazine ran photographs, and in 1952 a park ranger, Louis G. Kirk, recorded the length, width and direction of the trails. Over the years people proposed strong winds, slick mud, algae, ice, and even pranksters. In 1995, Professor John Reid led a team of students from Hampshire College and the University of Massachusetts Amherst that found trails from different stones lined up so closely that they must have moved together, which pointed toward sheets of ice. A 1996 study showed that wind is sped up as it blows over a flat, smooth playa. All of these were pieces of the answer the Norris team finally watched happen in 2013 and 2014.

Protect the playa

Racetrack Playa is fragile. In 2013 park officials investigated the theft of several rocks, and tire tracks from people driving onto the wet clay can last for years. If you visit, the National Park Service asks you to stay off the playa when it is wet, never drive on it, and leave every rock where it is. The road to the Racetrack is long, rough and remote, so check current road conditions with the park before you go.
