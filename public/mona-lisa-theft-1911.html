The Day the Mona Lisa Walked Out of the Louvre

Strange Crime · September 29, 2026

In August 1911, on a Monday when the Louvre in Paris was closed to visitors, the museum was quiet. A handyman named Vincenzo Peruggia, who had worked at the museum and even helped build protective glass cases for its paintings, walked in wearing a white workman's smock.

He took the Mona Lisa off the wall, slipped it out of its frame, and left through a door that a plumber helpfully unlocked for him. Sources disagree on some details, such as whether he hid overnight in a closet and how he carried the painting out, so we leave those out.

Nobody noticed

Paintings were taken down for cleaning and photographing all the time, so nobody thought much of the empty spot on the wall. It took more than a day for the theft to be reported.

When the news broke, it became a sensation. Thousands of people lined up to look at the empty space where the painting had been. Police questioned several people, including a young Pablo Picasso, who was cleared.

Two years in a trunk

For about two years, the Mona Lisa stayed hidden in Peruggia's small room in Paris, in a trunk. In late 1913, he tried to sell it to an art dealer in Florence, Italy. The dealer contacted the director of the Uffizi Gallery, and Peruggia was arrested. Sources differ by a day on the date of the arrest (December 11 or 12, 1913), so we give only the year.

The painting was returned to the Louvre in January 1914.

The theft made her famous

The Mona Lisa was already admired by art lovers, but the theft turned her into a global celebrity. Newspapers around the world printed her image on their front pages. History.com reports that at least 120,000 people came to see her in her first two days back on the wall.

The painting went back to the Louvre and has stayed there ever since.

Who was Vincenzo Peruggia?

Pietro Vincenzo Antonio Peruggia was born on October 8, 1881, in Dumenza, a small village in the Italian Alps near the Swiss border. He moved to Paris in 1908 and for a while worked at the Louvre, cleaning and reframing paintings. Part of his job was building protective glass cases for important works. That meant he knew the building, knew the staff routines, and knew exactly how the Mona Lisa's case was put together.

Why he said he did it

Peruggia claimed his motive was patriotic. He said he wanted to return the painting to Italy, believing Napoleon had taken it, and he had learned while working at the Louvre about the art Napoleon's armies carried out of Italy. That belief was mistaken in this case: Leonardo da Vinci brought the Mona Lisa to France himself and it was acquired by the French king Francis I, long before Napoleon. Other theories suggest money was part of it. At his trial in Florence in 1914, the court accepted his patriotic motive to some degree. He was sentenced to one year and fifteen days in jail, was hailed as a hero by many Italians, and on appeal served only about seven months.

The road home

Before the painting went back to Paris, it was shown in Italy, including at the Uffizi Gallery in Florence, where crowds came to see it. It then returned to the Louvre in January 1914. Peruggia later served in the Italian army in World War I, was held as a prisoner of war by Austria-Hungary for two years, married, had a daughter, and returned to France, where he worked as a painter and decorator.

Lessons the Louvre learned

The theft exposed how loose museum security was in 1911: a former worker could walk in on a closed day, remove one of the world's most important paintings from four iron hooks, and walk out. Today the Mona Lisa sits behind thick protective glass, under constant watch, and remains the most visited painting in the Louvre. In a strange way, the man who stole her helped make her the most famous painting on Earth.
