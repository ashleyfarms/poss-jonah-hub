Hiroo Onoda: The Soldier Whose War Lasted Until 1974

Strange History · September 29, 2026

World War II ended in 1945. For Japanese officer Hiroo Onoda, it did not end until 1974.

In December 1944, Onoda was sent to Lubang, an island in the Philippines about 75 miles southwest of Manila. He was an army intelligence officer, and his orders from Major Yoshimi Taniguchi were to carry on guerrilla warfare and not to surrender.

Why he did not come out

When the war ended, leaflets were dropped saying so. Onoda took them for a trick. He later said the leaflets had mistakes in them, so he judged they were an American plot. Over the years, search parties came looking, and he treated those as traps as well.

He stayed in the jungle for twenty-nine years. He had three companions at first. One surrendered in 1950, one was killed in 1954, and the last was killed in 1972 in a clash with Philippine forces.

The cost

This is not a lighthearted story, and it should not be told as one. By most accounts, Onoda and his men killed about thirty people over the years, including local villagers they believed were enemies. Sources give slightly different numbers. Many people on Lubang never forgave him.

Found by a young traveler

In early 1974, a Japanese adventurer named Norio Suzuki went to Lubang looking for Onoda, and found him within four days. Onoda told him he would only stop on orders from his commanding officer.

So the government tracked down Major Taniguchi, who by then was working as a bookseller. He flew to Lubang in March 1974 and gave Onoda the order to stand down in person.

The surrender

On March 11, 1974, Onoda handed over his sword at Malacañang Palace in Manila. Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos pardoned him, and gave the sword back.

Onoda returned to Japan to a hero's welcome. He later wrote a memoir, No Surrender: My Thirty-Year War, and lived in Brazil for a time before he died in 2014 at age 91.

Notes on the sources

Accounts vary on the exact number of people killed and on how long he was in the jungle ("29 years" in the BBC and New York Times, "almost 30 years" in the Guardian). We use the more cautious wording where they differ.

Who he was before Lubang

Hiroo Onoda was born on March 19, 1922, in Kamekawa, in Wakayama Prefecture, Japan. As a teenager he went to work for a trading company in Wuhan, China, and in 1942 he was conscripted into the Imperial Japanese Army. He was trained as an intelligence officer at the Futamata branch of the army's Nakano School, which taught guerrilla tactics and intelligence work. On December 26, 1944, he was sent to Lubang. His mission was to wreck the island's airstrip and harbor pier ahead of the Allied invasion and to attack any enemy planes or boats that tried to land, and his orders said plainly that he was not to surrender or take his own life.

Three companions, one by one

Onoda's last companions were Private Yūichi Akatsu, Corporal Shōichi Shimada and Private First Class Kinshichi Kozuka. The first leaflet they saw announcing the war's end, in October 1945, read "The war ended on 15 August. Come down from the mountains!" They decided it was enemy propaganda. Akatsu walked away from the group in September 1949 and surrendered in March 1950; the others treated this as betrayal and grew even more careful. In 1952, letters and family photographs were dropped from the air urging them to give up. They did not. Shimada was killed in May 1954 in a clash with a Philippine Army unit, and Kozuka was killed by local police in October 1972 during one of the raids in which the two men burned villagers' rice harvests. After that, Onoda was alone for about a year and a half.

Life after the jungle

Onoda found modern Japan hard to settle into. In April 1975, following his older brother, he moved to Brazil and became a cattle rancher in Mato Grosso do Sul, and he married in 1976. In 1984 he returned to Japan and started a nature camp for young people, while spending part of each year in Brazil. Brazil later honored him with the Santos-Dumont Merit Medal in 2004 and honorary citizenship of Mato Grosso do Sul in 2010. He died of heart failure from pneumonia in Tokyo on January 16, 2014.

How he is remembered

In Japan, Onoda was for many a symbol of duty and endurance; when he died, Japan's chief cabinet secretary said Onoda's return had reassured him the war was really over. In the Philippines, the memory is very different. Filipino media interviewed villagers who said Onoda and his men had killed up to thirty civilians over the years, deaths that Onoda did not mention in his autobiography. When he visited Lubang in 1996, a scholarship fund was offered to the local school. Both sides of the story are true at once: a man who held to an order with astonishing loyalty, and families on Lubang who paid for it. Watch our short video on Onoda.
