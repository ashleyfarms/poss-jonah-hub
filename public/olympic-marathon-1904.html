The Strangest Olympic Marathon: St. Louis, 1904

Strange History · September 29, 2026

The marathon at the 1904 Summer Olympics in St. Louis is often called one of the strangest races in Olympic history. Nearly everything that could go wrong did.

The setup

The course ran about 24.85 miles (40 km) on dusty roads in temperatures around 90°F. There was almost no water. Runners were allowed one stop, a well at about mile 12. Thirty-two men started the race, and only fourteen finished.

The car ride

Fred Lorz, an American runner, cramped up around mile nine. He hopped in a car and rode for roughly eleven miles, waving at spectators. When the car broke down, he started running again and entered the stadium first. The crowd cheered.

Then people found out. Lorz said it was a joke. He was banned for life, though the ban was lifted, and he won the Boston Marathon the next year.

The nap

Félix Carvajal, of Cuba, ran in street shoes and street clothes. Sources differ on the details, but he ate some apples along the way, felt sick, and lay down for a nap. He still finished fourth.

The winner

Thomas Hicks of the United States was the first to finish legitimately, in about 3 hours and 29 minutes. In the late stages his trainers gave him doses of strychnine mixed with egg whites, and brandy. Strychnine is a strong poison, used mainly to kill rats. In small doses it was widely used as a stimulant at the time. It was not safe then and it is not safe now, and Hicks needed help to get across the line.

Why it matters

The 1904 race is remembered as a reminder of how little was known then about heat and hydration. Please do not try any of this at home.

Why there was so little water

The water shortage was not just bad luck. James Edward Sullivan, a chief organizer of the 1904 Games, set up only one water stop, a well at about the halfway mark, even though the race was run in about 90°F (32°C) heat on unpaved roads. His stated reason was to study "purposeful dehydration." One runner, Félix Carvajal, managed to get a drink from a water tower about six miles in. The result was the worst finish rate in Olympic marathon history, 14 of 32, and the slowest winning time ever, 3:28:53, a full 29 minutes slower than the next slowest Olympic winning time.

Dust, traffic and dogs

The course was not closed. Runners had to dodge delivery wagons, trolley cars, trains and people walking dogs, while officials' cars kicked up thick clouds of dust. John Lordan, who had won the 1903 Boston Marathon, became violently ill after 10 miles and dropped out. Sam Mellor, the 1902 Boston winner, was leading at halfway but became disoriented and quit after 14.5 miles. William Garcia of the United States was found unconscious beside the road with serious internal injuries caused by breathing the dust, one of the race's near-fatalities.

The first African Olympians

Two men from South Africa, Len Taunyane and Jan Mashiani, ran the marathon and are remembered as among the first Black Africans to compete in the Olympic Games. Taunyane finished ninth even though, according to accounts of the race, a dog chased him about a mile off course. Mashiani finished twelfth. Both were in St. Louis as part of an exhibit connected to the World's Fair being held at the same time.

A debate that is still going

Historians are still arguing about the final results. One historian, Clément Genty, has pointed to the two official 1905 reports of the race and argued that Hicks got a car escort and was physically carried by officials over part of the course, which would raise questions about the official order of finish. The official results have not been changed, but it is a good reminder that even "settled" history can be reopened when someone goes back to the original documents.
