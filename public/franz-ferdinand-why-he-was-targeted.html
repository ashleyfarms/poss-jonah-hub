Why Franz Ferdinand Was Targeted: The Road to Sarajevo, 1914

Strange History · November 17, 2026 · By Poss Jonah

In October 1914, a thin young man stood in a Sarajevo courtroom. Asked what he believed in, Gavrilo Princip said he was a Yugoslav nationalist. Asked how he meant to get what he wanted, he answered, "By means of terror." Four months earlier he had shot Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the throne of Austria-Hungary. By the time he spoke, Europe was already at war.

The shooting is usually told as the spark of World War I. This article looks at the question that comes before it: why that man? The answer runs through forty years of Balkan history, a secret society led by an army spy chief, a group of poor teenagers, and an archduke who, surprisingly, was one of the strongest voices for peace in Vienna. This article is the companion to the first episode of the Poss Jonah series World War One: A Deep Dive, written for curious readers about 11 and up.

1878: a province on loan

The Balkans are the mountainous region of southeastern Europe between Italy and Turkey. For centuries much of it was ruled by the Ottoman (Turkish) Empire. In 1875 Christian farmers in Bosnia and Herzegovina rose up against Ottoman rule, setting off a chain of wars. In 1878 the great powers met in Berlin to redraw the map. Two decisions mattered for our story: Serbia became fully independent, and Article 25 of the Treaty of Berlin let Austria-Hungary occupy and run Bosnia, even though it still belonged to the Ottomans on paper.

Austria-Hungary built railways, schools and government buildings, but the old land system mostly stayed. Christian farmers, most of them Serbs, kept working land owned largely by Muslim landlords and handing over a big share of each harvest. That produced a lot of angry young men.

1903: a king overthrown

In neighboring Serbia, King Alexander Obrenović was friendly with Austria-Hungary and unpopular at home. On the night of June 10, 1903, army officers broke into the palace in Belgrade and killed the king and Queen Draga. One leading plotter, Captain Dragutin Dimitrijević, was badly wounded that night and carried bullets in his body for the rest of his life. Friends called him "Apis," after the sacred bull of ancient Egypt, because he was big and strong like a bull. The new king, Peter Karađorđević, turned Serbia toward Russia and France, and a group of officers came away believing they had made a king and could steer the country.

The Pig War and the annexation crisis

Serbia was a farming country and sold much of its livestock, especially pigs, through Austria-Hungary. In 1906, when Serbia bought cannons from France instead of Austria, Vienna banned Serbian livestock. This trade war is really called the "Pig War." It backfired: Serbia built its own meat-packing plants and found new buyers, and came out stronger and angrier.

In 1908 the foreign ministers of Austria-Hungary and Russia met secretly at Buchlau castle. No notes were kept, and historians still argue about exactly what was agreed. On October 6, 1908, Austria-Hungary announced it was annexing Bosnia, making it officially part of the empire. Serbia erupted in protest. Russia, recently defeated by Japan, was not ready to fight, and when Germany backed Austria, Russia backed down. On March 31, 1909, Serbia had to promise in writing to be a good neighbor. Russia resolved never to be humiliated that way again, a promise that shaped its choices in 1914.

The Black Hand

On May 9, 1911, nine men in Belgrade, most of them army officers, signed the rules of a secret society called "Unification or Death." Newspapers called it the Black Hand. Apis became its most powerful member. Its surviving rulebook reads like a spy novel: members were known by numbers, could never resign, and swore an oath to take its secrets to the grave. Its seal showed a skull-and-crossbones flag, a knife, a bomb and a bottle of poison. In the best-known English translation, it says the group "prefers terrorist action to intellectual propaganda."

By 1913 Apis was head of intelligence for the Serbian army. But the Black Hand and the Serbian government were not the same thing. Prime Minister Nikola Pašić saw the officers as a rival government, and by spring 1914 relations were so bad there was talk of another coup.

Young Bosnia and Gavrilo Princip

"Young Bosnia" was a loose movement of students, mostly teenagers from poor farm families, who believed Austria had no right to rule Bosnia. Their hero was a student named Bogdan Žerajić, who in 1910 fired at the Austrian governor of Bosnia, missed, and took his own life. A pamphlet turned him into a legend.

Gavrilo Princip was born in 1894 to a poor Serb farming family in western Bosnia; six of his parents' nine children died as babies. In 1912, after being expelled from school in Sarajevo for joining protests, he walked about 170 miles (280 km) to Belgrade. When the Balkan War began that fall, he tried to join a Serbian volunteer unit and was turned away as too small and weak. One of the men who rejected him, Major Vojislav Tankosić, was a founder of the Black Hand, and two years later Tankosić's people supplied Princip's pistol. At trial Princip said: "I am the son of a peasant; I know how people live in the villages, and that is why I wanted revenge."

Means and opportunity

In spring 1914 newspapers announced that Franz Ferdinand would visit Bosnia in June to watch army maneuvers. In Belgrade, Princip and two friends, Nedeljko Čabrinović and Trifko Grabež, decided to kill him. Through a railway worker named Milan Ciganović they reached Tankosić. According to the investigation and their own testimony, they received six bombs from a Serbian army arsenal, four pistols, money and cyanide, which was meant for taking their own lives afterward so they could not be made to talk. Border officers passed them along a secret route into Bosnia that historians call "the tunnel." In Sarajevo, Danilo Ilić recruited three more young men.

Age turned out to matter enormously. Under Austro-Hungarian law, no one under twenty at the time of the crime could be executed. Princip was 27 days short of his twentieth birthday, and that is the only reason he was not hanged.

The target

Franz Ferdinand was the nephew of Emperor Franz Joseph and became heir after the emperor's only son died and then Franz Ferdinand's own father. He was famously stubborn and not well liked at court. He was also obsessed with hunting: his diaries recorded more than 270,000 animals shot, most of them birds. He had married for love; his wife, Sophie, was a countess, not royal enough under family rules, so their children were barred from the throne.

Politically, he disliked Hungary's ruling class and favored reorganizing the empire to give its Slavic peoples a stronger place, though nobody knows which plan he would actually have used. To young nationalists, that was dangerous: a future emperor who treated South Slavs better could undercut their argument that they could never get a fair deal inside Austria-Hungary. Princip said as much at his trial.

He was also a brake on war. The army chief, General Franz Conrad von Hötzendorf, pushed again and again for war with Serbia; historian Hew Strachan counts twenty-five such proposals in 1913 alone. Franz Ferdinand repeatedly said no, saying he wanted "not a single plum tree, not a single sheep from Serbia," as historian Christopher Clark quotes him. The assassins removed one of the few men in Vienna with the power and the will to restrain the generals.

The date

Franz Ferdinand visited Sarajevo on Sunday, June 28, 1914: Vidovdan, St. Vitus's Day, the anniversary of the Battle of Kosovo in 1389. Most historians think Vienna chose the date simply to fit the end of maneuvers. To Young Bosnia, an Austrian heir in uniform in Sarajevo on that day looked like an insult.

What historians say

Historians still disagree about blame. Vladimir Dedijer saw the plot mainly as a revolt by young Bosnians, with the Black Hand supplying tools. Fritz Fischer argued that Germany's leaders used the crisis to launch a war they wanted. Christopher Clark stresses Apis's network but spreads responsibility across Europe's capitals, which he describes as sleepwalking into disaster. Margaret MacMillan argues that war was not inevitable and that a handful of leaders made choices that could have gone differently. Open questions remain, including how large Apis's role was (he claimed in 1917 to have organized it, while on trial for his life) and what Serbia's prime minister knew. On July 13, 1914, Austria's own investigator reported finding no proof that the Serbian government took part.

The trial ran from October 12 to 23, 1914. Ilić and two men who helped smuggle the weapons were executed in 1915. Princip, Čabrinović and Grabež, too young to hang, received twenty years and died of tuberculosis in prison before the war ended.
