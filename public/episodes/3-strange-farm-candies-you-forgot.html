Well, That's Weird: History

3 Strange Farm Candies You Forgot

True, odd moments from history, told short and checked against at least two sources.

The story

Before candy corn ruled Halloween, kids were snacking on candy turnips. Around the 1880s, candy makers molded mellowcreme into farm shapes and sold to farm and country kids all year round. One, turnips. Two, chestnuts. Three, clover leaves. Back then, about half of America's workers were on farms. So what really happened? Candy corn stuck around, but the candy turnips not so much.

Candy for a farm country

In the 1880s, the United States was still a mostly rural country. Candy makers knew it, and they shaped sweets to look like things their customers saw every day on the farm. The standard history of candy corn notes that in the late 1800s, "butter cream" candies molded into nature shapes were popular, including chestnuts, turnips and clover leaves. These are the three forgotten candies in this episode.

How they were made

These candies belong to a family called mellowcreme. The base is mostly sugar and corn syrup, cooked into a sweet slurry, sometimes with honey or other flavorings. The candy gets its shape through a method called starch casting. Trays are filled with cornstarch, a mold presses shaped hollows into the starch, and the hot candy is poured into them. The candy sets in the starch, is shaken clean, and can get a glaze for shine. In the early days much of this was done by hand, with workers carrying buckets of hot candy and pouring each layer. Later, a machine called a mogul automated the process.

Why candy corn survived

Candy corn was invented in the 1880s by George Renninger, who worked for the Wunderle Candy Company in Philadelphia, and Wunderle was the first to make it commercially in 1888. The Goelitz Confectionery Company, known today as Jelly Belly, started making it in 1898. Its original name was "Chicken Feed," and it was sold with farm-friendly packaging, which you can read more about in our episode Candy Corn Was Sold as Chicken Feed.

What set candy corn apart from the turnips and clover leaves was its look: three bright stripes of yellow, orange and white that resemble a kernel and fit the harvest season. Around the 1950s, as trick-or-treaters started getting individually wrapped candy, candy corn settled in as a fall and Halloween tradition. The turnips, chestnuts and clover leaves faded away.

Their cousins are still around

Mellowcreme never really left. Brach's sells "Mellowcreme" pumpkins, the best-selling candy pumpkin, made the same starch-cast way. Stores also sell harvest mixes with pumpkins, acorns and other shapes alongside candy corn. In 1985, when Congress was holding hearings on daylight saving time, candy makers reportedly put candy pumpkins on every senator's seat, hoping for more daylight on Halloween. Daylight saving time did not cover Halloween until the 2005 law that extended it to the first Sunday in November, starting in 2007.

So the next time you see a bag of candy corn, remember that it once shared a shelf with candy turnips. One of them became a Halloween icon. The other one was a turnip.

Quick facts

  • Era: late 1800s, when about half of American workers were on farms
  • Forgotten shapes: turnips, chestnuts and clover leaves
  • Candy type: mellowcreme, made by pouring hot sugar syrup into cornstarch molds
  • The survivor: candy corn, first made commercially by Wunderle in Philadelphia in 1888 and sold as "Chicken Feed"
  • Still on shelves: candy pumpkins and harvest mix
