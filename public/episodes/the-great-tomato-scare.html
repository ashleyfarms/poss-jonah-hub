Well, That's Weird: History

The Great Tomato Scare

True, odd moments from history, told short and checked against at least two sources.

The story

A fruit from the Americas

The tomato is a native of western South America, and it was domesticated in Mexico long before Europeans arrived. Spanish explorers carried it across the Atlantic in the 1500s. In Spain and Italy, cooks took to it fairly quickly. The Italian physician Pietro Andrea Mattioli mentioned the plant in his herbal of 1544, and about ten years later he named the fruits pomi d'oro, or "golden apples," which is where the modern Italian word pomodoro comes from.

Farther north, the welcome was cooler. Botanists noticed right away that the tomato belongs to the nightshade family, the same group of plants as mandrake and deadly nightshade, both of which really are poisonous. The tomato plant's leaves and stems contain compounds that can upset the stomach, and its strong smell did not help. For many gardeners in northern Europe, the tomato became an ornamental curiosity rather than a food.

"Rank and stinking"

One of the most influential voices was John Gerard, an English barber-surgeon and gardener. His Herball, published in 1597, was one of the first English books to describe the tomato. Gerard knew the fruit was eaten in Spain and Italy, but he described the plant as having a "rank and stinking savour" and treated it as unwholesome. His book was widely read for generations, and his opinion helped keep the tomato off many English tables for a long time. That attitude crossed the Atlantic with English colonists.

Was America really afraid of tomatoes?

The popular story says Americans believed tomatoes were deadly until well into the 1800s. The real picture is more mixed. Some people in the early United States were wary of them, and many grew tomatoes only for their looks. But others ate them without fuss. Thomas Jefferson, who had eaten tomatoes in Paris, grew them in his garden at Monticello, and tomato recipes appear in American cookbooks of the 1820s, including Mary Randolph's The Virginia House-Wife. Food historians, especially Andrew F. Smith, have argued that the idea of a nationwide "poison tomato" panic was exaggerated after the fact. The suspicion was real in some places and among some people, but it was never universal.

The Salem courthouse legend

The most famous tomato story of all is set in Salem, New Jersey. As it is usually told, in 1820 a gentleman farmer named Colonel Robert Gibbon Johnson announced that he would eat a basket of tomatoes on the steps of the Salem County Courthouse to prove they were safe. A crowd, sometimes said to number around 2,000, gathered to watch him die. A woman fainted, a band played, and Johnson ate one tomato after another. When he did not drop dead, the crowd cheered, and the tomato's reputation was saved.

It makes a terrific scene. The trouble is that there is no record of it from Johnson's own lifetime.

Who was Robert Gibbon Johnson?

Johnson was a real and prominent man. He was born in 1771 and died in 1850. He was a farmer, judge, soldier and local politician, and he was interested in horticulture. He served as president of the New Jersey Horticultural Society, and in 1839 he published a history of the early settlement of Salem. A good deal of written material about him survives. None of it from his lifetime mentions a dramatic tomato-eating stunt.

How the story grew

Andrew F. Smith traced the legend in a 1990 article in the journal New Jersey History titled "The Making of the Legend of Robert Gibbon Johnson and the Tomato." According to his research, the earliest written claim linking Johnson to the introduction of the tomato in Salem dates only to the early twentieth century. The courthouse story itself was popularized much later by Joseph Sickler, a Salem postmaster and local historian. Sickler told the tale to the writer Harry Emerson Wildes, who put it in his 1940 book The Delaware. In 1946 Stewart Holbrook retold it in Lost Men of American History and added dialogue. Then, in 1949, the CBS radio program You Are There broadcast a dramatized re-enactment, with Sickler as a consultant. After that, the story seemed to be everywhere.

Johnson did grow tomatoes, and southern New Jersey really did become a major tomato-growing region, shipping produce to the big markets of Philadelphia and New York. Over time, though, the tall tale grew bigger than the man. Salem even staged costumed re-enactments of the courthouse scene in the 1980s.

How the tomato actually won

The tomato's rise in America did not hinge on one brave man. By the 1830s it had become fashionable. Newspapers and doctors began praising it, and for a few years there was even a craze for tomato-based patent medicines sold as cures for indigestion and other complaints. The fad for tomato pills faded, but the tomato stayed. Through the rest of the century, cookbooks filled with tomato recipes, and canning made tomatoes available all year. Today the tomato is one of the most widely grown vegetables in the world, and New Jersey still celebrates its tomatoes as a point of pride.

What really happened

The fear of tomatoes was real in parts of Europe and among some early Americans, and it came from a reasonable observation: the plant is related to some truly poisonous ones. But the belief was never as universal as later stories claimed. The Salem courthouse stunt, as it is usually told, has no support in records from Johnson's lifetime. It appears to be a twentieth-century legend that a good storyteller, a book and a radio show turned into "history."
