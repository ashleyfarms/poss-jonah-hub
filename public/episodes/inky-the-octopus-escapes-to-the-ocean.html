Well, That's Weird: History

Inky the Octopus Escapes to the Ocean

True, odd moments from history, told short and checked against at least two sources.

The story

An octopus from a crayfish pot

In 2014, a fisherman found a male common New Zealand octopus in a crayfish pot about half a kilometer north of Napier Port, near Pania Reef, on the east coast of New Zealand's North Island. The octopus had some scars and injuries to his arms. He was taken to the National Aquarium of New Zealand in Napier, a seaside aquarium that faces the ocean along Marine Parade.

A few months later, a naming competition was held, and the winning name was Inky, a nod to the dark ink octopuses can squirt to escape predators. At the aquarium, staff gave Inky toys and puzzles to keep him busy, as is common for captive octopuses, which are known for being curious and easily bored. He shared a tank with another octopus called Blotchy.

The great escape

One night in early 2016, Inky disappeared. When staff arrived in the morning, his tank was missing an octopus, and there were wet tracks leading across the floor.

According to the aquarium's account, staff had left the lid of the tank slightly open after maintenance work the previous day. Sometime during the night, Inky apparently climbed up to the gap and squeezed through it. He then crawled down the outside of the tank and across the floor, a distance of a few meters, until he reached a drain. The drainpipe, about 15 centimeters (6 inches) wide, ran roughly 50 meters and emptied into Hawke Bay. Inky is believed to have slipped into it and made his way out to the open sea.

Staff also considered another route: an overflow pipe at the top of the tank that also connected to the drain. Either way, the aquarium concluded that Inky had escaped on his own and had not been stolen. Blotchy stayed behind in the tank.

Going public

The aquarium did not announce the escape right away. When the story finally became public about three months later, in April 2016, it spread around the world. News outlets including the BBC, The New York Times, CNN and The Guardian covered it. In New Zealand, Inky's sudden fame was compared to that of famous animal celebrities such as Happy Feet, the emperor penguin that wandered onto a New Zealand beach in 2011.

The aquarium's manager described Inky to reporters as an especially curious octopus. The aquarium said it did not plan to replace him.

How does an octopus fit through a drain?

Inky's escape seems impossible for an animal his size, but it makes sense once you understand octopus anatomy. Octopuses have no internal or external skeleton. The only hard part of an octopus's body is its beak, a parrot-like structure made of a tough material similar to fingernails, located in the center where its arms meet. As long as an opening is larger than the beak, an octopus can usually squeeze its soft body through it. A large octopus can slip through a gap only a few centimeters wide.

Octopuses can also survive for a short time out of water. They breathe with gills, which need water to work, but they can hold some water in their mantle cavity and move across damp surfaces for brief periods. Inky's trip across the aquarium floor would have been short enough to survive.

Clever and curious

Octopuses are famous for their intelligence. They have the largest brains of any invertebrates relative to body size, and about two-thirds of their neurons are in their arms rather than their central brain. In laboratories and aquariums, octopuses have been seen opening jars, solving puzzles, recognizing individual people and squirting water at keepers or lights they dislike. Escape stories like Inky's are not unique. Aquariums around the world have reported octopuses climbing out of tanks, sneaking into neighboring tanks for a meal and returning, or getting into pipes. For this reason, many aquariums use tight-fitting lids and special barriers on octopus tanks.

New Zealand has had other famous octopuses. In 2014, an octopus called Ozymandias at an aquarium in Wellington attracted attention for how quickly it could open a screw-top jar.

What happened to Inky?

Inky was never seen again. Common octopuses usually live only about one to two years, and they die a few months after breeding. In January 2017, a researcher at the University of Otago told reporters that Inky had most likely died of old age by then. Others suggested he may have found his way to one of the reefs along the Hawke's Bay coast and mated there before the end of his natural life. Nobody knows for sure.

A story that lived on

Inky's escape inspired children's books, including Inky's Great Escape and Inky the Octopus, and the aquarium received stories and drawings about him from children overseas. Inky's former tank was later home to a moray eel.

What really happened

Inky was a real octopus at the National Aquarium of New Zealand in Napier. One night in early 2016, after a lid was left slightly open, he squeezed out of his tank, crossed the floor and slipped down a narrow drainpipe that led to the sea. With no bones, an octopus can fit through any gap bigger than its beak, and Inky used that trick to take the shortcut home. He was never seen again, but his escape made him one of the most famous octopuses in the world.
