Well, That's Weird: History

Jack-o'-Lanterns Were Turnips First

True, odd moments from history, told short and checked against at least two sources.

The story

Before the pumpkin

Every October, porches across North America fill up with carved pumpkins. But the jack-o'-lantern did not start with pumpkins. In Ireland, Scotland and parts of England and Wales, the older Halloween lantern was carved from a root vegetable: a turnip, a swede (what Americans call a rutabaga) or a mangelwurzel, a large beet grown for animal feed. People hollowed it out, carved a face into the side, and set a candle or a glowing coal inside.

Surviving examples and old photographs show that these lanterns could look genuinely frightening. A turnip is small, hard and lumpy, and carving it takes patience and a sharp knife. The faces tended to come out gaunt and lopsided, with deep-set eyes and jagged mouths, more skull than smiley face.

A ghost turnip in a museum

The National Museum of Ireland keeps a famous example in its Irish folklife collection: a plaster cast of a carved turnip lantern, often called the "ghost turnip." The original lantern came from County Donegal and dates to around the start of the twentieth century. It was donated to the museum's collection in 1943. The cast preserves the hollow eyes and grim mouth of a lantern that, when lit on a dark country road, must have been unsettling.

Ireland's Schools' Collection adds more evidence. Between 1937 and 1939, the Irish Folklore Commission asked schoolchildren across the country to collect customs and stories from older relatives and neighbors. Entries from several counties describe Halloween turnip lanterns: a turnip scooped out, a face cut into it, a candle set inside, and the lantern carried around or placed in a window. Some children called it "Jack o' the lantern."

Where the name comes from

Long before it meant a carved vegetable, "jack-o'-lantern" was an English name for a mysterious light. People in marshy country sometimes reported faint, flickering lights over bogs and wetlands at night, also known as will-o'-the-wisps or, in Latin, ignis fatuus, "foolish fire." Folklore said the lights led travelers astray. The Oxford English Dictionary records the phrase used for these lights in the 1650s and 1660s, and also as a name for a man carrying a lantern or a night watchman. The dictionary's earliest example of "jack-o'-lantern" meaning a lantern carved from a turnip or pumpkin dates to 1837.

The legend of Stingy Jack

A popular Irish folk tale ties the two ideas together. In the usual version, a crafty, hard-drinking man called Stingy Jack tricks the Devil more than once, trapping him and making him promise not to claim Jack's soul. When Jack finally dies, heaven will not take him because of his wicked life, and the Devil, bound by his promise, will not let him into hell either. The Devil sends Jack off into the dark with a single burning coal to light his way. Jack puts the coal inside a hollowed turnip and has wandered the earth with it ever since, as "Jack of the Lantern."

Like many folk tales, the story exists in several forms, and nobody can say exactly when or where it began. In 1836, the Dublin Penny Journal published a long story about "Jack-o'-the-Lantern," though it did not describe the lantern as a carved vegetable.

Why carve a lantern at all?

Halloween falls on the eve of All Saints' Day, and in Gaelic-speaking regions the season overlapped with the old festival of Samhain, which marked the end of the harvest and the start of winter. Folk belief held that it was a night when spirits and fairies were especially active. According to later accounts, carved lanterns were sometimes said to represent spirits, sometimes used to frighten neighbors as a prank, and sometimes set in windows to keep harmful spirits away. Historians caution that much of what is said about ancient Halloween customs comes from nineteenth- and twentieth-century writers, and that the deeper origins of the lantern are uncertain. What is well recorded is that carved turnip and mangelwurzel lanterns were a Halloween tradition in parts of Ireland and Britain by the nineteenth century.

The move to pumpkins

Millions of Irish and Scottish emigrants crossed the Atlantic in the nineteenth century, especially during and after the Great Famine of the 1840s. They brought their Halloween customs with them. In North America, pumpkins were native, cheap and plentiful in autumn. They were also far bigger and softer than turnips, which made them much easier to hollow out and carve.

Americans were already carving pumpkins before Halloween was a big holiday. The poet John Greenleaf Whittier, in his 1850 poem "The Pumpkin," remembered boys carving "wild, ugly faces" into pumpkins lit by a candle, in a poem about Thanksgiving rather than Halloween. By 1866, a newspaper in Kingston, Ontario, described young people celebrating Halloween with pumpkins carved into glowing heads and faces. As Halloween grew into a major North American holiday in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the carved pumpkin became its symbol.

Turnips today

In recent years, museums and heritage groups in Ireland have encouraged people to try carving a traditional turnip lantern again. Galway City Museum, for example, has published step-by-step instructions. Anyone who tries quickly learns why the pumpkin won: turnips are tough to scoop out, and they take a long time to carve. The result, though, is a lantern with a lot more personality.

What really happened

The jack-o'-lantern is older than the pumpkin version most people know. Carved root-vegetable lanterns were a Halloween custom in Ireland and Britain, and the name itself first described ghostly lights over the bogs. Irish and Scottish emigrants carried the custom to North America, where the big, soft native pumpkin made carving easier. Your porch pumpkin is the New World upgrade of an old Irish turnip.
