Well, That's Weird: History

Candy Corn Was Sold as "Chicken Feed"

True, odd moments from history, told short and checked against at least two sources.

The story

A candy shaped like a farm

Candy corn is one of the most recognizable sweets in North America: a small, waxy, triangular kernel striped yellow, orange and white. Today it is sold almost entirely around Halloween, and people tend to either love it or hate it. But candy corn started out with no connection to Halloween at all. It was a farm-themed novelty candy from the late nineteenth century, and one of its early brand names was "Chicken Feed."

The butter-cream craze

In the 1880s, American confectioners were making a type of soft candy often called butter cream, a sugary mixture that could be poured into molds and set into shapes. Candy makers used it to produce little sweets in the forms of everyday things, especially from the farm and the garden. Shapes such as chestnuts, turnips, clover leaves and pumpkins were popular.

The reason was simple marketing. In the late 1800s, a large share of Americans still lived and worked on farms, and candy that looked like the crops and animals they knew was familiar and fun. Corn was an especially fitting subject, since it was one of the country's most important crops. At the time, much of the corn grown in the United States was fed to livestock, so the idea of corn as chicken feed was immediately recognizable to customers.

Who made the first candy corn?

The traditional story credits George Renninger, who worked for the Wunderle Candy Company in Philadelphia, with creating the three-color kernel in the 1880s. Wunderle is often said to have begun producing it around 1888. The evidence for Renninger's role comes largely from later accounts and company histories rather than detailed records from the time, so food historians treat the exact origin with some caution. What made his candy stand out from other butter-cream shapes was its striking layered coloring, which made it look like a real corn kernel.

Goelitz and the rooster box

The candy corn story is closely tied to the Goelitz family. Gustav Goelitz, a German immigrant, had started a candy business in the Midwest. In 1898 the Goelitz Confectionery Company began making candy corn, and it marketed the candy under the name "Chicken Feed," with packaging that featured a rooster. Old advertising and boxes from the early twentieth century show the company still using farm imagery and slogans like "King of the Candy Corn Fields." The company later moved to California and is known today as Jelly Belly, the jelly bean maker, which still produces candy corn.

How it was made

In the early days, candy corn was made by hand, and it was hard work. Workers cooked sugar, corn syrup and water into a thick slurry in large kettles, then added fondant for texture and marshmallow for a softer bite. The hot mixture was poured into trays of cornstarch that had been pressed with kernel-shaped molds. Each color had to be poured in a separate pass to create the three stripes. Because the work was seasonal and labor-intensive, candy corn was typically made from spring to late autumn.

The basic method, called cornstarch molding, is still used today, but machines took over most of the pouring decades ago. Modern candy corn is made from sugar, corn syrup, gelatin, honey, flavorings, food coloring and a shiny confectioner's glaze.

From penny candy to Halloween

For the first half of the twentieth century, candy corn was not a Halloween specialty. It was a cheap, popular bulk candy, sold loose by weight or by the penny in general stores and candy shops all year round.

Its link to Halloween grew in the years after World War II. As trick-or-treating spread across the United States in the late 1940s and 1950s, families began handing out individually wrapped candy and small packages of treats. Candy corn's yellow, orange and white stripes fit neatly with autumn harvest colors, and candy companies increasingly advertised it in October. Over time, the candy that began as a year-round farm novelty became a symbol of the season.

Candy corn today

Candy corn is now made in enormous quantities. The largest producer is Brach's, part of the Ferrara Candy Company, which dominates the market during the Halloween season. Industry figures have put annual U.S. production in the tens of millions of pounds, which works out to billions of individual kernels, most of them sold in the weeks before Halloween. The National Confectioners Association has even promoted 30 October as National Candy Corn Day.

Candy makers have also spun off many variations, including "harvest corn" with a chocolate-flavored brown end, pastel versions for Easter, red-and-green versions for Christmas, and novelty flavors such as caramel apple and pumpkin spice. The classic three stripes remain the best seller.

Love it or hate it

Candy corn has an unusually divided reputation. Polls and surveys about Halloween candy regularly find it near the top of both the most-loved and least-loved lists, and food writers have called it one of the season's most debated sweets. Fans enjoy its honey-vanilla flavor and soft chew. Critics compare it to sweetened wax. Either way, it has outlasted nearly all of the other farm-shaped butter-cream candies of the 1880s.

What really happened

Candy corn began as one of many butter-cream novelty candies shaped like farm produce, aimed at a largely rural country. It is traditionally credited to George Renninger at Wunderle Candy in Philadelphia, and by 1898 the Goelitz company was selling it as "Chicken Feed" in a box decorated with a rooster. For decades it was an everyday penny candy. Only after trick-or-treating took off in the mid-twentieth century did it become the Halloween treat known today.
