Well, That's Weird: History

The Bear Who Went to War (Wojtek)

True, odd moments from history, told short and checked against at least two sources.

The story

A cub on the road through Iran

In the spring of 1942, thousands of Polish soldiers and civilians were leaving the Soviet Union for Iran. Most of them had been deported to Soviet labor camps and settlements after the Soviet invasion of eastern Poland in 1939. When Germany attacked the Soviet Union in 1941, the two governments agreed to release the Poles so they could form a new army under General Władysław Anders. That force, often called Anders' Army, crossed the Caspian Sea to Iran, then headed south and west to join the British in the Middle East.

On 8 April 1942, on the road from the port of Pahlavi toward Tehran, a group of these Poles met a young Iranian boy carrying a bear cub. The cub's mother had reportedly been shot by hunters. Accounts differ on the details, but the usual version is that an 18-year-old refugee named Irena Bokiewicz fell for the little bear and persuaded a Polish lieutenant, Anatol Tarnowiecki, to buy him. The cub spent his first months in a Polish refugee camp near Tehran. In August 1942 he was handed over to a transport unit that later became the 22nd Artillery Supply Company of the 2nd Polish Corps. The soldiers named him Wojtek, a familiar form of the old Slavic name Wojciech, which is usually translated as "happy warrior" or "smiling warrior."

Raised by a company of soldiers

The cub was too young to eat solid food at first, so the men fed him condensed milk from an old vodka bottle. As he grew, he moved on to fruit, honey, marmalade and syrup. The soldiers' accounts say he loved beer, took cigarettes (which he tended to eat rather than smoke), wrestled with the men for fun and learned to salute. On cold nights he sometimes slept among the soldiers for warmth. He had assigned caretakers, and he traveled with the company in a crate on the back of a truck.

The 22nd Company moved through Iraq and Syria to Egypt. Wojtek became a well-known sight at every camp, and soldiers from other units came by to see the bear who lived like one of the men. By the time the company reached Italy, he weighed around 90 kilograms (about 200 pounds) and could stand taller than a man on his hind legs.

How a bear joined the army

In early 1944 the 2nd Polish Corps was ordered to Italy to fight alongside the British Eighth Army. There was a problem: the British transport ships did not allow pets or mascots. The soldiers' solution was to enlist Wojtek. He was entered on the company roll as a private, with a name, a rank, a pay book and a serial number, and he sailed for Italy as a soldier rather than an animal.

Monte Cassino

The 2nd Polish Corps arrived in time for the Battle of Monte Cassino, one of the hardest fights of the Italian campaign. Allied forces had attacked the German defensive line around the hilltop monastery several times since January 1944 without breaking through. In May 1944, the Polish Corps was given the task of taking the heights. After heavy losses, Polish troops raised their flag over the ruins of the monastery on 18 May 1944.

The 22nd Company's job was to keep the guns supplied. According to the soldiers' accounts, Wojtek watched the men unloading ammunition and began to copy them, carrying heavy crates of artillery shells from the trucks and never dropping one. Historians treat the details with some caution, since most of the story comes from the men who loved him, but at least one British soldier later recalled seeing a bear carrying crates of ammunition. The company was proud enough of the story to adopt a new badge: a bear carrying an artillery shell. Wojtek was later promoted to corporal.

Scotland and Edinburgh Zoo

When the war ended in 1945, many soldiers of the 2nd Polish Corps could not safely go home, because Poland was now under a Soviet-backed government. Large numbers were brought to Britain instead. In 1946 the 22nd Company, Wojtek included, was moved to Winfield, a former airfield near the village of Hutton in the Scottish Borders. The bear became a local celebrity, and the Polish-Scottish Association made him an honorary member.

The company was demobilized in November 1947, and a soldier bear could not simply be discharged into civilian life. Wojtek was given to Edinburgh Zoo, where he lived for the rest of his life. Former Polish soldiers often visited. Keepers and visitors noticed that he perked up when people called to him in Polish, and some of his old comrades are said to have climbed into his enclosure to wrestle with him as they had during the war. He appeared on the BBC children's program Blue Peter. Wojtek died at the zoo on 2 December 1963, at about 21 years old.

How he is remembered

Wojtek's story has outlived nearly all of the soldiers who knew him. A bronze statue by the sculptor Alan Beattie Herriot, showing the bear walking beside a Polish soldier, was unveiled in West Princes Street Gardens in Edinburgh in 2015. Kraków unveiled its own statue in Jordan Park on 18 May 2014, the 70th anniversary of the capture of Monte Cassino. There are memorials in Duns in the Scottish Borders, in Cassino in Italy, and in several Polish cities, and a street leading to the Poznań zoo is named after Corporal Wojtek. The Imperial War Museum in London holds material about him, and his story has been retold in books, plays and an animated short film.

What we know and what we don't

The broad outline of Wojtek's life is well documented: the purchase in Iran, his life with the 22nd Artillery Supply Company, his official enlistment, his time in Scotland and his years at Edinburgh Zoo. The most famous detail, the bear hauling shells under fire at Monte Cassino, rests mostly on soldiers' memories and is harder to verify. Even so, the company's own badge shows how they saw him: not as a pet, but as one of their own.
