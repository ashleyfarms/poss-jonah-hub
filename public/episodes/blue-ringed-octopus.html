Blue-Ringed Octopus

Real animals that sound made up. Every claim in these episodes is checked against published science or museum sources.

The story

Small, shy and dangerous

At first glance, a blue-ringed octopus is not much to look at. It is small, with a body about the size of a golf ball and arms that bring its total span to roughly 12 to 20 centimeters (5 to 8 inches). Most of the time it is a mottled yellowish or tan color that blends in with rocks, sand and seaweed. It spends much of its life tucked into crevices, empty shells and tide pools, sometimes piling up small stones at the entrance of its den.

But this little octopus carries one of the most powerful venoms in the sea. Blue-ringed octopuses are considered among the most venomous marine animals in the world, and a single bite can be life-threatening to a human. They are not aggressive and rarely bite people. Almost every serious case involves someone picking one up.

Where they live

Blue-ringed octopuses belong to the genus Hapalochlaena. Four species are formally recognized, including the greater blue-ringed octopus, the southern blue-ringed octopus and the blue-lined octopus, and researchers suspect there may be several more not yet described. They live in the Pacific and Indian Oceans, from Japan through Southeast Asia to Australia. In Australia they are found all around the coast, including in shallow rock pools near popular beaches, which is why Australian museums and safety campaigns warn visitors about them.

They live in shallow water, in tide pools, rocky reefs, seagrass beds and coral reefs, and they mainly hunt small crabs, shrimp and other crustaceans, along with small fish.

The flash of blue

When a blue-ringed octopus feels threatened, its appearance changes dramatically. Its skin turns brighter yellow and dozens of rings, often 50 to 60 across its body and arms, flash an electric, iridescent blue. The rings can appear in about a third of a second. This is a warning display, telling predators that the octopus is dangerous.

Scientists studying the greater blue-ringed octopus found that the blue color does not come from pigment. Each ring contains layers of reflective cells called iridophores, which are stacked in a way that reflects blue-green light across a wide angle, so the rings look bright from many directions. Normally, muscles above each ring contract to hide it. When the octopus is alarmed, those muscles relax and others around the ring contract, exposing the reflective layer. Dark pigment cells around each ring expand at the same time, making the blue stand out even more. A 2012 study in the Journal of Experimental Biology described this system in detail.

Tetrodotoxin

The blue-ringed octopus's venom contains tetrodotoxin, the same powerful toxin found in pufferfish, some newts and a few other animals. Tetrodotoxin blocks sodium channels in nerve cells, which stops nerves from sending signals to muscles. In prey such as crabs, that quickly causes paralysis. In humans, a large enough dose can paralyze the muscles used for breathing.

Scientists believe the octopus does not make tetrodotoxin entirely on its own. Bacteria living in its salivary glands are thought to produce or help produce the toxin, which the octopus then uses. Tetrodotoxin has also been found in other parts of the animal's body, and in its eggs.

What a bite is like

One reason blue-ringed octopuses are so dangerous is that their bite can be almost painless. The beak is small, and a person may not realize they have been bitten. Symptoms can start within minutes and may include numbness or tingling around the mouth, lips, face or tongue, followed by weakness, difficulty swallowing and speaking, blurred vision and loss of coordination. In severe cases, paralysis spreads to the muscles that control breathing. A person may remain conscious and aware even while unable to move or breathe on their own.

There is no antivenom for blue-ringed octopus bites. Treatment focuses on keeping the person breathing, with rescue breathing or mechanical ventilation in a hospital, until the toxin wears off, which may take many hours. Medical and diving-safety organizations stress that with fast first aid and support for breathing, people can survive. Deaths from blue-ringed octopus bites are rare, and in documented cases victims were usually handling the animal. Anyone who may have been bitten should get emergency medical help immediately.

Life cycle

Blue-ringed octopuses live short lives, usually around two years or a little longer. A female lays a single clutch of eggs in her lifetime, often a few dozen, depending on the species. In many species she carries the eggs under her arms and guards them for weeks or months, without eating. Shortly after the eggs hatch, she dies. The young grow quickly and are ready to reproduce within about a year.

Despite their reputation, blue-ringed octopuses are not considered threatened, and they play a normal role in their reef and rock-pool ecosystems as small predators of crustaceans.

How to stay safe

Blue-ringed octopuses are not interested in people and will usually try to hide or swim away. Safety advice is straightforward:

Do not pick up any octopus, especially a small one in a rock pool, and teach children not to handle unfamiliar sea creatures. Be careful when reaching into crevices, turning over rocks or picking up shells, which may be used as hiding places. Wear sturdy footwear when exploring tide pools. If someone may have been bitten, call emergency services right away and be ready to support their breathing until help arrives.

What really happened

The blue-ringed octopus is a small, shy animal that spends most of its time hiding and hunting crabs in tide pools and reefs from Japan to Australia. When disturbed, it flashes bright blue rings as a warning. Its venom contains tetrodotoxin, which can paralyze and has no antivenom, but bites are rare and nearly always happen when someone handles one. Look, admire the rings from a distance, and never touch.
