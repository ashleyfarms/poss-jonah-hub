Sovereign Citizens Pt 2: "I'm Not Driving, I'm Traveling"

Odd, non-graphic true-crime stories and courtroom curiosities.

The story

Courts in Canada and Australia describe the claim this way. A license is for commercial driving, but private traveling is an absolute right. Yeah, courts say no.

Now, there's a real right to travel. The Supreme Court says you can cross state lines freely, but a federal appeals court said there's no right to drive, and back in 1915, the Supreme Court upheld state rules for motor vehicles. Am I being detained? Yes, says the Supreme Court. A traffic stop counts, and in some states you are required to give your name.

In court, one federal judge said jurisdiction is not a child's game of magic talismans, like gold fringe on a flag. And the Seventh Circuit said sovereign claims should be rejected summarily. A real right to travel? Yes. A right to skip the license? No. Pay the ticket or hire a real lawyer.

The argument in plain words

The "right to travel" argument goes like this: the word "driver" in the law only means someone driving for hire or commerce, so a private person using their own car is merely "traveling," which is a constitutional right that cannot be licensed, registered or taxed. People who believe this sometimes remove their license plates, hand officers a stack of paperwork, refuse to show a license, and ask "Am I being detained?" over and over. Videos of these stops are popular online, and they almost always end the same way.

What the real right to travel is

There really is a constitutional right to travel in the United States. The Supreme Court has recognized it in cases like Shapiro v. Thompson (1969) and Saenz v. Roe (1999). It protects your right to move from one state to another, to be treated as a welcome visitor while you are there, and to be treated the same as other residents if you move. It is about crossing state lines, not about a particular way of getting there.

States have regulated motor vehicles for over a century. In Hendrick v. Maryland (1915), the Supreme Court upheld a state's power to require registration and licensing for cars on its roads, noting that motor vehicles are dangerous and that states can regulate them for public safety. Federal appeals courts have said repeatedly that there is no fundamental right to drive a car, and that a license requirement does not violate the right to travel. You can still walk, ride a bus, take a train, fly or ride with someone else.

"Am I being detained?"

During a traffic stop, the answer is yes. The Supreme Court has held that a traffic stop is a seizure under the Fourth Amendment, which means the driver is detained for the length of the stop. In Brendlin v. California (2007), the Court held that passengers are seized too. In Hiibel v. Sixth Judicial District Court of Nevada (2004), the Court upheld state laws that require a person who is lawfully stopped to give their name. Many states also require drivers to show a license, registration and proof of insurance on request.

Gold fringe and magic words

Some sovereign arguments claim that a courtroom flag with gold fringe turns the court into a military or admiralty court with no power over civilians, or that saying certain words in a certain order removes a person from the court's reach. Federal courts have rejected all of it. The Seventh Circuit Court of Appeals, in United States v. Benabe (2011), wrote that no matter what a person calls themselves, they are not beyond the jurisdiction of the courts, and that these theories "should be rejected summarily, however they are presented." Judges in Canada and Australia have written the same thing about the "traveling" claim specifically.

What actually happens

People who try this at a traffic stop typically end up with more trouble than a ticket: a towed car, charges for driving without a license or registration, and sometimes charges for resisting or obstructing an officer. In court, filing these arguments can bring sanctions for frivolous filings. None of that is worth avoiding a ticket.

If you think a ticket or a stop was unfair, you have real options. You can contest the ticket in traffic court, ask for a hearing, file a complaint with the police department, or talk to a licensed attorney. Those tools work. Magic words do not. Missed Part 1?
