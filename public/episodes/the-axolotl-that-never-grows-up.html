Well, That's Weird: Animals

The Axolotl That Never Grows Up

Real animals that sound made up. Every claim in these episodes is checked against published science or museum sources.

The story

The salamander that stays young

Most salamanders live a double life. They hatch in water as larvae with feathery external gills, then go through metamorphosis, absorbing their gills, developing lungs and heading onto land as adults. The axolotl of Mexico mostly skips that second act. It keeps its frilly gills, its finned tail and its aquatic lifestyle for its entire life, yet it still grows to full size and breeds. Scientists call this trait neoteny: keeping juvenile features into adulthood.

The axolotl (Ambystoma mexicanum) is a kind of mole salamander, closely related to the tiger salamander of North America. Its name comes from Nahuatl, the language of the Aztecs, and is often linked to Xolotl, an Aztec god associated with fire, lightning, death and transformation. In one Aztec story, Xolotl tried to escape sacrifice by changing shape, finally becoming an axolotl in the water.

What an axolotl looks like

An adult axolotl usually reaches about 23 centimeters (9 inches) long, though some grow larger. It has a broad head, small lidless eyes, a wide mouth that seems to curve into a permanent smile, and three pairs of branching gills sweeping back from each side of its head like feathery antlers. The gills are lined with fine filaments that increase their surface area for absorbing oxygen from the water. Axolotls also have lungs and can gulp air at the surface, and they can absorb some oxygen through their skin.

Wild axolotls are usually dark brown, olive or gray, often with speckles, which helps them blend in with the muddy bottoms of canals. The pale pink, white-bodied axolotls with bright red gills that many people know from pet shops and cartoons are a color variety, a form called leucistic, which is common in captivity but rare in the wild. Captive breeding has also produced golden, albino and other color forms.

Why it never grows up

The reason axolotls stay in their larval form has to do with hormones. In most amphibians, metamorphosis is triggered by thyroid hormones. Axolotls normally do not produce enough of the signals that set this process in motion, so their bodies never get the message to transform. In laboratories, scientists have shown that giving an axolotl thyroid hormone can force it to metamorphose into a land-dwelling salamander that looks much like a tiger salamander. This is rarely done outside research, because the change is stressful and often shortens the animal's life.

Biologists think neoteny made sense in the axolotl's original home: cool, high-altitude lakes surrounded by land that offered little advantage to a terrestrial salamander. Staying in the water, where food was plentiful, was a winning strategy.

A champion of regeneration

The axolotl is famous among scientists for its ability to regrow lost body parts. If an axolotl loses a leg, it can grow a complete new one, with bones, muscles, nerves and skin in the right places, usually without forming a scar. Researchers have also reported that axolotls can regenerate parts of their tails and spinal cords, gills, parts of the heart, and parts of the eyes and brain.

This ability has made the axolotl one of the most studied amphibians in the world. Researchers want to understand how its cells "know" what to rebuild, how it avoids scarring and why its immune response helps rather than blocks healing. In 2018, scientists published the axolotl's genome. At about 32 billion base pairs, roughly ten times the size of the human genome, it was the largest animal genome sequenced up to that time. The hope is that lessons from the axolotl could one day help improve wound healing and tissue repair in people, though that remains a long-term goal.

Home in the canals of Xochimilco

In the wild, axolotls are found only in a small area near Mexico City. They once lived in a network of high-altitude lakes and wetlands in the Valley of Mexico, including Lake Xochimilco and Lake Chalco. Over centuries, those lakes were drained to control flooding and make room for the growing city. Today, wild axolotls survive mainly in the remaining canals of Xochimilco, a district famous for its colorful boats and chinampas, the "floating gardens" built by farmers since Aztec times.

Critically endangered

The wild axolotl is in serious trouble. The International Union for Conservation of Nature lists it as Critically Endangered. Its habitat has shrunk and suffered from pollution and urban runoff, and introduced fish such as tilapia and carp eat axolotl eggs and young and compete for food. Surveys in Xochimilco have found sharp declines in axolotl numbers over recent decades, and some estimates suggest only a small number of adults remain in the wild.

Conservation groups and scientists in Mexico are working with chinampa farmers to restore clean water refuges in the canals, using natural filters and barriers to keep out invasive fish. Ironically, while wild axolotls are close to disappearing, they are common in captivity, kept in laboratories around the world and as pets. Captive animals, however, are not a substitute for a healthy wild population, which is why protecting Xochimilco matters.

A cultural icon

The axolotl has become a popular symbol in Mexico and beyond. It appears in art, toys and video games. In 2021, the Bank of Mexico issued a redesigned 50-peso note featuring an axolotl. Mexico City has an axolotl museum, and the animal has become a mascot for efforts to protect the city's remaining wetlands.

What really happened

The axolotl is a real salamander that keeps its childhood looks for life, breathing through feathery gills and staying in the water while still growing up and reproducing. It can regrow lost limbs and repair other tissues without scarring, which makes it a star of scientific research. In the wild, it survives only in the shrinking canals around Mexico City, where it is critically endangered.
