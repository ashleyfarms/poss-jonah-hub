Well, That's Weird: Cryptids

Whalers Found a "Baby Sea Serpent" Inside a Whale

Cryptids are creatures people report but science has never confirmed. In each episode Poss Jonah tells where the legend comes from, what people say they saw, and what a sensible explanation might be, then lets you decide.

The story

In 1937, whalers in Canada opened up a sperm whale and found what folks called a baby sea serpent. And they took its picture, Meet Cadborosaurus, Caddy for short, named after Cadboro Bay in Victoria, British Columbia. Witnesses describe a horse-like head, a long neck, humps in a row behind it, and flippers front and back.

Over 200 sightings were collected over a century. That 1937 carcass came out of the whale at Naden Harbour. A sample went to the provincial museum and the director's best guess? An unborn baleen whale. Yeah, whatever. In 1943 two police officers saw a huge sea serpent with a horse-like head. But then one grabbed binoculars and it was just a big bull sea lion leading six others all bobbing in a line.

So, one critter, or a lot of different critters, all getting called Caddy? Take a look at that old photo and you decide. What are them Canadians up to up there is what I want to know. Well, That's Weird.

Meet Caddy

Cadborosaurus takes its name from Cadboro Bay in Victoria, British Columbia, plus saurus, the Greek word for lizard. The nickname "Caddy" was given by a Victoria journalist, Archie Wills. Witnesses describe a serpent-like animal with a horse-like head on a long neck and a row of humps or coils behind it, with small front flippers and either back flippers or a fan-shaped tail. There are reports along the Pacific coast of North America, from Saanich Inlet near Victoria up into Alaska and down as far as San Francisco Bay. More than 300 claimed sightings have been counted over about 200 years.

The Naden Harbour carcass

In 1937, workers at the whaling station at Naden Harbour on Haida Gwaii cut open a sperm whale and found a long, odd-looking body in its stomach. They laid it out on a board and photographed it, and that picture is still the most famous "Caddy" image. A sample was sent to the provincial museum in Victoria, and the museum's director thought it was most likely an unborn baleen whale. Nobody can recheck that today because the specimen did not survive.

The sea lion parade

One of the best reminders of how sightings happen comes from 1943. Two police officers, Inspector Robert Owens and Staff Sergeant Jack Russell, saw what looked like a huge sea serpent with a horse-like head in the Strait of Georgia. Then Russell looked through binoculars. It was a big bull sea lion leading six others in a line. As they surfaced and dived one after another, their backs looked like one long body with humps. To the naked eye, it was a perfect sea monster.

Other suspects

Scientists have suggested a long list of real animals: conger eels, humpback whales, elephant seals, oarfish, basking sharks and sea lions. The giant oarfish can reach about 11 meters (36 feet) and swims with a wavy motion. Rotting basking sharks are famous for turning into fake "plesiosaur" carcasses as the jaw and fins fall away. In 1934, a 30-foot body on Henry Island near Prince Rupert was examined by marine biologist Neal Carter and identified as a basking shark.

Two researchers, Paul LeBlond and Edward Bousfield, argued that no known animal matches all the sightings, especially the flippers at both ends. Zoologist Darren Naish replied that this assumes all the reports describe one animal, when it is simpler to read them as many different things that all got called Caddy. Several First Nations traditions along the coast describe sea creatures too, though scholars warn against assuming they all describe the same thing.

You decide

So there is a real photo, a real whaling station, a real museum opinion, and a real case of sea lions fooling two cops. Whether there is one Caddy or many is up to you.
