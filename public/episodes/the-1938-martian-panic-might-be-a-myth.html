Well, That's Weird: History

The 1938 Martian Panic Might Be a Myth

True, odd moments from history, told short and checked against at least two sources.

The story

The broadcast

On the evening of Sunday, 30 October 1938, the night before Halloween, CBS radio aired an episode of The Mercury Theatre on the Air. The series was led by Orson Welles, then 23 years old and already well known for his stage work in New York. That week's show was an adaptation of H. G. Wells's 1898 novel The War of the Worlds, about a Martian invasion of Earth.

The script was written mainly by Howard Koch, with producer John Houseman and others reworking it in the days before the show. They moved the story from Victorian England to present-day America and told much of it as a series of breaking news bulletins. The program opens with what sounds like an ordinary broadcast of dance music. It is interrupted by reports of strange explosions on Mars, then of a meteor-like object landing on a farm in Grovers Mill, New Jersey. A reporter on the scene describes creatures emerging from the object and attacking with a heat ray. Within the hour, the "news" reports Martian machines marching across New Jersey and poison gas over New York City.

Welles introduced the show as a radio play at the start, and CBS announced during and after the broadcast that it was fiction. But the realistic bulletins, the use of real place names and the calm voice of authority made it unusually convincing for listeners who tuned in partway through.

The headlines

The next morning, newspapers across the United States reported that the broadcast had set off a wave of terror. The New York Times ran a front-page story headlined "Radio Listeners in Panic, Taking War Drama as Fact." Other papers described people fleeing their homes, crowding into churches and wrapping wet towels around their faces against Martian gas. Within three weeks, newspapers had published thousands of articles about the broadcast and its aftermath. Politicians called for tighter government control of radio, and the Federal Communications Commission looked into the program, although it took no formal action against CBS.

That version of events became the standard story: a young Orson Welles fooled the nation and caused a mass panic. It has been repeated in textbooks, documentaries and anniversary articles ever since.

The case for a smaller scare

Some media historians argue that the panic was far smaller than the newspapers claimed. Two of the best-known skeptics, Jefferson Pooley and Michael J. Socolow, laid out the case in a widely read article on the broadcast's 75th anniversary in 2013.

Their first point is the size of the audience. On the night of the broadcast, the C. E. Hooper company, the main radio ratings service of the day, telephoned 5,000 households as part of its regular survey. Only 2 percent of those reached said they were listening to the radio play. Most people with their radios on were tuned to NBC's Chase and Sanborn Hour, a hugely popular variety show starring the ventriloquist Edgar Bergen, which aired in the same time slot. Some CBS affiliates had also skipped the Mercury Theatre that night in favor of local programs.

Their second point is about who was telling the story. In 1938, newspapers were losing advertising money to radio, a younger and fast-growing rival. A story about radio spreading terror and confusion gave papers a chance to portray the new medium as irresponsible. Reporters also gathered much of their evidence the same night, often by phone, which made it easy for a few dramatic anecdotes to stand in for a national picture.

What the evidence shows

There is no doubt that some people were frightened. Police stations, newspapers and radio stations in several places, especially in New Jersey and New York, received many more calls than usual during and after the broadcast. Telephone operators later recalled panicked callers. Of the nearly 2,000 letters sent to Welles and the FCC afterward, roughly a quarter came from frightened listeners or people who said they had seen panic.

But calling the police or a newspaper to ask whether something is true is not quite the same as running into the street. Historians who have studied the newspaper coverage closely, including W. Joseph Campbell, have found that most reports of people taking drastic action came in small numbers and were hard to confirm. Researchers at the time found no rush of shock patients at a Newark hospital, and New York hospitals reported no extra admissions that night.

The Princeton study

The most influential estimate of the scare came from a Princeton University study led by the psychologist Hadley Cantril, published in 1940 as The Invasion from Mars. Cantril estimated that about six million people heard the broadcast, that about 1.7 million believed it was real news and that about 1.2 million were frightened or disturbed.

Critics have pointed out problems with those numbers. Cantril's audience estimate was more than twice as high as any other at the time. Many of his interviews took place after weeks of newspaper coverage, which could have shaped what people remembered. And his study grouped listeners who said they were "excited," "disturbed" or "frightened" together, without separating those who were truly fooled from those who knew it was a play but found it unsettling.

Why the story lasted

The idea of a nation in panic fit worries of the late 1930s, when war was approaching in Europe and many people were anxious about the power of radio and propaganda. It also made a great story about Orson Welles, who went on to direct Citizen Kane in 1941. Each retelling helped cement the legend.

What really happened

On 30 October 1938, a cleverly written radio play convinced some listeners, for a while, that something terrible was happening. Some of them were genuinely scared, and some called the police or their neighbors. How many is still debated. The evidence suggests the audience was much smaller than often claimed, and that the image of a whole country in hysteria owes a lot to sensational newspaper coverage. The broadcast was real, the fear was real for some, and the nationwide panic may be mostly myth.
