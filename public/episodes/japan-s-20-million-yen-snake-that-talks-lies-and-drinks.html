Well, That's Weird: Cryptids

Japan's 20-Million-Yen Snake That Talks, Lies and Drinks

Cryptids are creatures people report but science has never confirmed. In each episode Poss Jonah tells where the legend comes from, what people say they saw, and what a sensible explanation might be, then lets you decide.

The story

A town in Japan has offered 20 million yen for a snake that supposedly talks, tells lies and likes to drink. So we're talking about a politician, yeah? All right, y'all, I'm fixing to mispronounce the heck out of some words. Meet the tsuchinoko, child of the hammer, a fat-bellied snake up to two and a half feet long with fangs and venom like a viper.

Now legend says it can jump three feet and spin around in midair. Some say it bites its tail and rolls like a wheel, like our old hoop snake, you know? In 1988, a councilman in the village of Shimokitayama launched a Tsuchinoko expedition. One million yen for one caught alive, about 200 people joined in.

They hunted till 1990 and found nothing. The village still opened a Tsuchinoko Park in 2023. Now in 2007, a dead tsuchinoko, something like that, shaped snake was reportedly found in imported hay on a farm, but the body went missing. So is this a real creature or a story that grew with every telling? Well, 20 million yen is still waiting.

Personally, I think I ought to be able to collect that because we got a whole bunch of these snakes in Washington, D.C., but you decide. This redneck can't say these words.

What a tsuchinoko is supposed to be

Tsuchinoko is usually translated "child of the hammer," because the creature is pictured as a snake with a short, fat middle, a bit like the head of a mallet. The name is most common in western Japan, including Kansai and Shikoku. In northeastern Japan the same kind of creature is called bachi hebi.

Descriptions put it at roughly 30 to 80 centimeters long (about 12 to 31 inches), with fangs and venom like a viper's. It is said to jump about a meter, and some stories say it can make a second jump while still in the air. Folklore adds three personality traits that make it famous: it can talk, it likes to lie, and it has a taste for alcohol. Some legends also say it grabs its own tail and rolls downhill like a wheel, which is exactly what the American "hoop snake" is supposed to do.

The 1988 expedition

In the late 1980s a wave of reported sightings swept Japan, centered on the village of Shimokitayama in Nara Prefecture. In 1988 a village council member, Kazuo Nozaki, launched a "Tsuchinoko Expedition." The prize was 1 million yen for a live tsuchinoko (about $7,800 at the time) and 300,000 yen for a piece of its skin. Around 200 people from all over the country joined in. The search ran until 1990 and turned up no evidence at all. The village still had fun with it: a Tsuchinoko Park opened there in 2023 to remember the hunt.

Other towns have posted bounties too. Records list a 2 million yen reward from Chikusa in Hyōgo Prefecture, which ended when the town merged into Shisō, and a 20 million yen reward from Yoshii in Okayama Prefecture. That 20 million yen figure is where the headline on this episode comes from.

The hay-bale snake

On April 4, 2007, a dead snake shaped like a tsuchinoko was reportedly found inside imported hay on a farm in Ōkura in the Tōhoku region. That sounds like the big break, but the body reportedly went missing, so nobody can examine it now. There is also a reported sighting of a 30-centimeter creature of the same shape on a mountain near Lake Towada.

Sensible explanations

Japan does have real, heavy-bodied snakes, and any snake that has just swallowed a big meal looks fat in the middle for a while. Skinks and other lizards with short legs can also look odd at a glance. A snake seen briefly in tall grass, or a known snake that has eaten a rodent, could easily grow into a "tsuchinoko" once the story gets told around town. Some writers have also pointed to imported lizards with thick bodies as a possible source of modern sightings.

The talking, lying and drinking parts are pure folklore, and that is part of the charm. The tsuchinoko is a little like a Japanese cousin of our hoop snake and jackalope: a creature that is half warning and half joke. The rewards are real, though, and nobody has collected one yet.

Quick facts

  • Name: tsuchinoko, "child of the hammer"; also bachi hebi in the northeast
  • Size in stories: about 12 to 31 inches
  • Famous search: Shimokitayama, Nara, 1988 to 1990, about 200 hunters, nothing found
  • Largest listed bounty: 20 million yen (Yoshii, Okayama)
