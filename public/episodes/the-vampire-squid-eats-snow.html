Well, That's Weird: Animals

The Vampire Squid Eats Snow

Real animals that sound made up. Every claim in these episodes is checked against published science or museum sources.

The story

A frightening name

In 1903, the German marine biologist Carl Chun described a strange animal collected during the Valdivia deep-sea expedition. It had dark reddish skin, enormous eyes and eight arms joined by a web of skin, with rows of fleshy spikes along the inside. Chun gave it the scientific name Vampyroteuthis infernalis, which translates roughly as "vampire squid from hell."

The name stuck, and so did the spooky reputation. But more than a century of research has shown that the vampire squid is not a vampire, not a fierce hunter and not even a true squid. It is a gentle, slow-moving drifter that makes its living by catching the slow rain of debris that falls through the deep ocean, known as marine snow.

Not a squid, not an octopus

The vampire squid belongs to its own order, Vampyromorphida, and it is the only living member. It shares features with both squids and octopuses but fits neatly into neither group. Scientists often describe it as a "living fossil," the last survivor of an ancient lineage of cephalopods whose relatives are known from fossils tens of millions of years old.

Adult vampire squid are small, reaching a maximum length of about 30 centimeters (1 foot). Their skin ranges from velvety black to reddish brown. They have two ear-like fins on the sides of the body, which they flap to swim. Relative to body size, their eyes are among the largest of any animal. A vampire squid about 15 centimeters long can have eyes around 2.5 centimeters across. Those huge eyes help it detect faint light in the deep sea.

Life in the oxygen minimum zone

Vampire squid live in tropical and temperate oceans around the world, usually at depths of roughly 600 to 900 meters (2,000 to 3,000 feet), where almost no sunlight reaches. Many of them live in a layer of water called the oxygen minimum zone, where dissolved oxygen is extremely low. Few predators and competitors can survive there.

The vampire squid is well adapted to this harsh environment. It has a very low metabolic rate, meaning it uses little energy. Its blood contains a pigment that binds oxygen efficiently, and it has large gills for its size. Its body has a jelly-like texture and is nearly neutrally buoyant, so it can hover in the water with little effort.

What it really eats

For a long time, scientists assumed that the vampire squid, like most cephalopods, hunted live prey. In 2012, Hendrik Hoving and Bruce Robison of the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute (MBARI) published a study that changed that picture. Using deep-diving remotely operated vehicles, they observed vampire squid in their natural habitat and also examined the stomach contents of preserved specimens.

They found that the vampire squid feeds on marine snow, a steady drizzle of organic particles that sinks from the sunlit waters above. Marine snow is made of things like dead plankton, bits of shells, fecal pellets and mucus. It is not glamorous, but in the food-poor deep ocean, it is a reliable meal.

Fishing with a sticky line

The vampire squid has two special filaments, separate from its eight arms, that it can extend far out into the water. According to the MBARI research, these thin filaments can stretch up to about eight times the animal's body length. As particles of marine snow drift down and stick to the filament, the vampire squid pulls it back in and wipes the food off with its arms.

The squid then mixes the collected particles with mucus produced by glands on its arms, forming small balls of food that it passes to its mouth. The researchers described this as a feeding strategy unique among cephalopods, since nearly all other squids and octopuses are active predators.

The pineapple pose

When threatened, the vampire squid has a dramatic defense. It flips its webbed arms up and back over its body, turning itself inside out like a cloak. In this position, often called the "pineapple" posture, its dark inner web covers it and the fleshy spines on its arms point outward. The pose makes it look larger and harder to grab, and it hides the squid's head and body.

Glowing in the dark

The vampire squid is covered with light-producing organs called photophores, small white discs that can glow and flash. The largest are at the tips of its arms and near its fins. It can control how bright the lights are and how long they last, from quick flashes to glows lasting several minutes. Scientists think these displays may confuse predators or help it avoid being seen.

Unlike many cephalopods, the vampire squid does not have an ink sac for squirting dark ink. In the black deep sea, dark ink would not help much. Instead, it can release a cloud of sticky, glowing mucus from its arm tips. The glowing cloud may distract an attacker long enough for the squid to slip away into the dark.

What really happened

The "vampire squid from hell" has one of the most frightening names in the ocean, but it does not drink blood or hunt aggressively. It is a small, slow, ancient animal that drifts in the low-oxygen depths, catching falling marine snow on sticky filaments and rolling it into little balls of food. When frightened, it hides under its own cloak and puts on a light show. Its name says vampire, but its lifestyle says gentle snow collector.
