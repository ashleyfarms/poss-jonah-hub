Well, That's Weird: Cryptids

A 15-Foot Beaked Sea Monster Washed Up in 1983

Cryptids are creatures people report but science has never confirmed. In each episode Poss Jonah tells where the legend comes from, what people say they saw, and what a sensible explanation might be, then lets you decide.

The story

In 1983, a 15-year-old boy found a 15-foot sea creature on a beach in Africa. He had a long beak, the creature, not the boy, 80 teeth and four flippers. That's important, four flippers. It was June 12th on Bungalow Beach in the Gambia. The boy, Owen Burnham, was on holiday with his family. He described it as dark on top, white underneath, with small eyes, a dome forehead, and a 5 foot tail with no fluke and no blowhole he could see.

But two fishermen were already cutting it off its head to sell to a tourist. Now who wants that for a souvenir? Well, who knows, but no specimen survived. The find on Bungalow Beach

Cryptozoologists call this one "Gambo." On June 12, 1983, fifteen-year-old Owen Burnham, a young wildlife enthusiast on holiday with his family from neighboring Senegal, came across a carcass on Bungalow Beach at Kotu in The Gambia. He estimated it at about 15 feet long, including a tail of about 5 feet. It was dark on top and white underneath, had a long beak with 80 cone-shaped teeth all about the same shape, small eyes, a domed forehead, nostrils near the tip of the beak, four paddle-like flippers without claws, and a tail with no fluke. He did not see a blowhole.

When he arrived, two local fishermen were already cutting off the head to sell to a tourist, a job that reportedly took about twenty minutes because the backbone was so thick. They told him it was a dolphin. Burnham, who spoke Mandinka, said locals called the animal kunthum belein, which he understood to mean "cutting teeth."

How the story spread

The carcass itself was not saved, so everything we know comes from Burnham's description and sketches. In 1986 BBC Wildlife published an interview with him, and zoologist and author Karl Shuker wrote about the case in a magazine called The Unknown, asking readers to help identify it. The nickname "Gambo" came later, from a 1993 Fortean Times piece titled "Gambo, the Beaked Beast of Bungalow Beach." Shuker has said he did not coin the name.

Why the details matter

Four flippers is the detail that makes people sit up. Whales and dolphins have two front flippers and a tail fluke, not four limbs. That is why some writers wondered about a surviving marine reptile, something like a pliosaur. But retellings have drifted over the years. Some versions say the body was found untouched and buried the next day; Shuker's own descriptions of the color changed between articles; and some later write-ups add damage to the hind flippers that Burnham did not describe. When a story is told for decades without a specimen, small changes pile up.

What it might have been

The beak, domed forehead and many similar teeth sound a lot like a beaked whale or a dolphin. Shepherd's beaked whale and Cuvier's beaked whale have both been suggested, though each has problems: Shepherd's lives in the southern oceans, and Cuvier's has very few teeth. Seals and manatee relatives have also been floated and rejected by Shuker. Skeptics point out that dead whales and dolphins bloat, lose skin and change shape fast, and that a decaying carcass can look nothing like the living animal. A "hind flipper" on a rotting body could be torn tissue rather than a limb. Without bones to study, nobody can settle it.

What we know for sure: a teenager saw something strange on a beach in 1983, described it carefully, and the head went home with a tourist. That alone is a pretty good campfire story.
