Well, That's Weird: Cryptids

Two Sisters Saw a Bird-Man Over a Church Tower

Cryptids are creatures people report but science has never confirmed. In each episode Poss Jonah tells where the legend comes from, what people say they saw, and what a sensible explanation might be, then lets you decide.

The story

On April 17th, 1976, two young sisters on holiday in Cornwall, England, said they saw a big feathered bird-man hovering over a church tower. Meet the Owlman of Mawnan, named for the little village where it was seen, over the tower of St. Mawnan's church. Now the girls were so scared, the story goes, that their dad cut the family vacation short.

And one of them reportedly drew it. But that July, two 14-year-old girls camping nearby said they saw "a big owl with pointed ears as big as a man," with glowing eyes and black pincer-like claws. More reports came in 1978, 1979, 1989 and 1995. But church towers are a favorite nesting spot for barn owls.

The place

Mawnan is a small village on the south coast of Cornwall, near the mouth of the Helford River. Its church, St Mawnan and St Stephen, stands among trees above the water. In Cornish the creature is called Kowanden; in English it is the Owlman, or the Owlman of Mawnan.

How the story began

Everything traces back to a man named Tony "Doc" Shiels, a performer and showman who in 1976 was involved in a series of "monster-raising" stunts. Shiels said he looked into a report from two young sisters on holiday, usually named as June and Vicky Melling. On April 17, 1976, they reportedly saw a large "feathered bird-man" hovering over the church tower, and their father was so upset by their account that he cut the holiday short. Shiels said one of the girls gave him a drawing, and he named the creature the Owlman. The story spread in a 1976 pamphlet about another local monster, Morgawr, the sea serpent of Falmouth Bay.

According to Shiels, on July 3 two 14-year-old girls, Sally Chapman and Barbara Perry, who already knew the story, were camping nearby and saw "a big owl with pointed ears, as big as a man," with glowing eyes and black, pincer-like claws. Later scattered reports were recorded in 1978, 1979, 1989 and 1995, and in 2000 people talked about a loud owl-like call heard at night in the churchyard at Mullion.

Three explanations

Barn owls. Writer Joe Nickell points out that church towers are a classic nesting place for barn owls. A pale owl lit from below, gliding silently at dusk, looks much bigger and stranger than it is.

An eagle-owl. Writer and broadcaster Lionel Fanthorpe suggested a Eurasian eagle-owl. These are among the largest owls in the world, with ear tufts and orange eyes, and escaped birds do turn up in Britain.

A hoax. Historian Gareth Medway suggested the whole story may have been a hoax by Shiels, who had a reputation for this kind of thing. Medway noticed that witnesses to Shiels' monsters tended to be connected to him or reported only to him, and could not be interviewed independently.

What we can say

There is no photograph, no feather and no body. There is a real church, a real tradition of owls nesting in towers, and a storyteller with a track record. The Owlman has since shown up in TV shows, a play and a film, which tells you it makes a great story either way. You decide.
