American Flying Squirrel: The Pink Glow

Real animals that sound made up. Every claim in these episodes is checked against published science or museum sources.

The story

Meet the southern flying squirrel. Just a couple of ounces, giant eyes, and it only comes out at night. North Carolina wildlife officials call it probably the most common mammal people never see. It doesn't truly fly. It stretches skin between its wrists and ankles and glides, steering with its legs and tail. Usually twenty to thirty feet, sometimes much farther.

In 2019, researchers reported that flying squirrels glow pink under UV light. A researcher spotted it by accident, when he aimed a UV flashlight at his Wisconsin bird feeder. Why? Scientists don't know yet. In winter, they pile into a nest together, sometimes ten or more, to stay warm. You can't see the glow without a UV light. But watch a bird feeder after dark, and you might meet your neighbors. Just enjoy the show from a distance.

Meet the neighbor you never see

The southern flying squirrel (Glaucomys volans) is one of three flying squirrel species in North America. It lives in hardwood and mixed forests across the eastern half of the continent, from southeastern Canada down to Florida, and there are separate populations in the highlands of Mexico, Guatemala and Honduras. It is small: about 8 to 10 inches long counting its flat, feathery tail. It has gray-brown fur on top, a creamy belly, and huge dark eyes built for the dark. Because it comes out only at night, plenty of people have flying squirrels in their backyard trees and never know it.

How the "flying" works

Flying squirrels do not flap or truly fly. They glide on a furry membrane called a patagium that stretches from the front legs to the back legs. Before a jump, a squirrel often leans out and sways its head from side to side, which scientists think helps it judge the distance to its landing spot. Once airborne, it spreads its legs so the membrane forms a square-ish wing and glides down at an angle of about 30 to 40 degrees, steering with its legs and tail. Right before landing it pulls its body up so it hits the trunk feet-first, then often scoots around to the far side of the tree, a handy move when an owl is following.

The pink glow

In 2019 a team of researchers published a paper in the Journal of Mammalogy reporting that the fur of North American flying squirrels glows pink under ultraviolet light. The discovery started by accident, when a researcher in Wisconsin shined a UV flashlight at a flying squirrel visiting his bird feeder. The team then checked museum specimens and live animals and found the glow in all three North American flying squirrel species, in males and females, across a wide range. This kind of glow is called biofluorescence: the fur absorbs UV light and gives it back off at a visible color.

Why do they glow? Nobody knows yet. Ideas include communication with other flying squirrels in low light, or blending in with lichens and other things that also fluoresce, which might confuse owls. Scientists have stressed these are guesses until someone tests them. Since that paper, researchers have reported UV glow in other mammals too, including platypuses and springhares, so flying squirrels have plenty of strange company.

Winter huddles and family life

Southern flying squirrels are social. In cold weather they pile into the same tree cavity or nest box to share warmth, and studies have found that a squirrel in a group can save about 30 percent more energy than one sleeping alone. Nest-box studies in Florida found groups of up to 25. They nest in woodpecker holes and natural cavities, and in summer they also build leaf nests. Females can have two litters a year, with two to seven babies each. The babies are born without fur, open their eyes at around four weeks, and are independent by about four months old.

They eat acorns, hickory nuts and beechnuts and stash food for winter, but they also eat insects, mushrooms, buds, and sometimes bird eggs and nestlings. Their enemies include owls, hawks, snakes, raccoons and house cats.

Watching them safely

If you want to see one, sit quietly near a bird feeder or a stand of oaks after full dark with a dim red light. Do not handle them. Flying squirrels in the eastern U.S. have been linked to a rare form of typhus spread through the fleas and lice they carry, and the CDC documented 39 such cases from 1976 to 2001. Enjoy the show from a distance.
