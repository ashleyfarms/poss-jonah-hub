Well, That's Weird: Animals

The Aye-Aye, Spooky Tapping Lemur

Real animals that sound made up. Every claim in these episodes is checked against published science or museum sources.

The story

A strange little primate

The aye-aye (Daubentonia madagascariensis) is a lemur, a primate found only on the island of Madagascar. It is also one of the oddest-looking primates on Earth. It has large, staring eyes, huge bat-like ears, shaggy dark fur with pale tips, a long bushy tail and hands with long, thin fingers. Its front teeth keep growing throughout its life, like a rodent's. Early European naturalists were so puzzled by it that they first classified it as a kind of squirrel.

The aye-aye is the world's largest nocturnal primate. An adult has a head and body roughly 36 to 43 centimeters (14 to 17 inches) long, plus a tail that is even longer. It spends most of its life high in the trees of Madagascar's forests, sleeping by day in a ball-shaped nest of leaves and twigs and coming out at night to search for food.

The name

The French naturalist Pierre Sonnerat was the first to use the name "aye-aye," in 1782. He wrote that it came from a cry of exclamation and astonishment. Some later writers suggested the name may have come from local people's surprised reaction to the animal, or from a Malagasy word, but its exact origin is uncertain. Its scientific name honors the French naturalist Louis-Jean-Marie Daubenton.

Tap, listen, gnaw

The aye-aye's most famous behavior is the way it finds food. Many of its favorite meals are wood-boring insect larvae, or grubs, hidden in tunnels inside tree branches. To find them, the aye-aye walks along a branch tapping on the wood with its long, skinny middle finger, as fast as about eight taps a second. As it taps, it turns its enormous ears toward the wood, listening for a change in the sound that reveals a hollow tunnel underneath.

This hunting method is called percussive foraging. When the aye-aye hears a promising spot, it gnaws through the bark and wood with its ever-growing front teeth, making a small hole. Then it slides its middle finger into the tunnel and hooks out the grub. The middle finger is extremely thin and bony and moves on a special ball-and-socket joint, so it can be bent and twisted in different directions to probe deep into holes.

Aye-ayes also use their teeth and fingers to open coconuts, seeds and other hard foods, scooping out the soft insides. They eat nectar, fungi and fruit as well.

A hidden sixth finger

In 2019, researchers led by Adam Hartstone-Rose at North Carolina State University reported a surprising discovery. While studying aye-aye hands, they found a tiny extra digit on each wrist: a small "pseudothumb" made of a bone and cartilage, with its own muscles. It can move in several directions and even has its own fingerprint. The researchers think it helps the aye-aye grip branches, since its other fingers are so specialized for tapping and probing. The find made the aye-aye one of the few primates known to have a sixth digit of this kind.

Life in the trees

Aye-ayes are mostly solitary. Each one travels widely through the forest canopy at night, sometimes covering several kilometers. They communicate with calls and scent marks. Females usually give birth to a single baby every two to three years, and young aye-ayes stay with their mothers for a long time while they learn to forage. In captivity, aye-ayes have lived for more than 20 years.

Feared and honored

Aye-ayes have long had a complicated relationship with people in Madagascar. In many communities, they are considered omens of bad luck or death. Some traditional beliefs say that if an aye-aye points its long middle finger at someone, that person will die, or that seeing one near a village means misfortune is coming. Because of these beliefs, aye-ayes have sometimes been killed on sight.

Attitudes are not the same everywhere, however. Researchers studying local beliefs have found that in some places aye-ayes are viewed with respect, and some people consider them to be the embodied spirits of ancestors. A 2025 study involving Oxford Brookes University described how views ranged from fear to reverence across different regions and communities. Conservationists say understanding these beliefs is important for protecting the species.

Endangered

The aye-aye is listed as Endangered by the International Union for Conservation of Nature. Its biggest threats are the loss and fragmentation of Madagascar's forests through logging, farming and other land clearing, along with hunting and persecution. Because aye-ayes reproduce slowly, their populations recover only gradually from losses.

Captive breeding programs have helped scientists learn more about aye-ayes and supported conservation efforts. The Duke Lemur Center in Durham, North Carolina, has one of the most successful aye-aye breeding programs in the world and offers public education about the species. In Madagascar, protected areas and community conservation programs work to preserve the forests aye-ayes depend on.

What really happened

The aye-aye looks like a creature from a ghost story, with its huge eyes, giant ears and long, spindly finger. In reality, it is an endangered lemur that has evolved a clever way to find food: tapping on branches, listening for hidden tunnels, gnawing a hole and fishing out grubs with its special finger. It even has a tiny sixth finger to help it hold on. Some people fear it and some honor it, but it is not a monster, just one of evolution's most unusual inventions.
