Well, That's Weird: History

Why Do We Dress Up on Halloween?

True, odd moments from history, told short and checked against at least two sources.

The story

The popular answer

Ask why people wear costumes on Halloween and you will often hear the same story. Long ago, it goes, the ancient Celts of Ireland and Scotland celebrated a festival called Samhain on 31 October. On that night the boundary between the living and the dead grew thin, and spirits roamed the land. People wore masks and disguises so the wandering dead would not recognize them, and that, the story says, is why we still dress up today.

It is a tidy explanation, and parts of it have real roots. But historians who study early Ireland are much more cautious about it than most Halloween articles are.

What we know about Samhain

Samhain was real. It was one of the major seasonal festivals of the Gaelic year, marking the end of the harvest and the start of winter, around the beginning of November. Medieval Irish literature, written down by Christian monks centuries after the pre-Christian period, describes Samhain as a time of gatherings and feasts, and as a time when supernatural beings could cross into the human world.

What those sources do not describe in any detail is ordinary people putting on costumes to hide from ghosts. Historian Lisa Bitel, who studies medieval Ireland, has written that almost no evidence survives of what the ancient Irish actually did on that night. Much of the familiar link between Samhain and modern Halloween costumes was built by nineteenth-century antiquarians and folklorists, who were eager to find ancient pagan roots for the customs they saw around them. Their theories were repeated so often that they came to sound like settled fact.

What the records do show

The trail becomes much clearer from the late Middle Ages onward, when written records of the season become common. Halloween is the evening before All Saints' Day on 1 November, followed by All Souls' Day on 2 November, when Christians prayed for the dead. By the fifteenth century, there was a custom in parts of Britain of sharing "soul cakes" around this time. In a practice called souling, poor people and children went from door to door, offering prayers or songs for the souls of a household's dead relatives in exchange for food or money. William Shakespeare refers to it in The Two Gentlemen of Verona, written in the early 1590s, when a character is accused of whining "like a beggar at Hallowmas."

In Scotland and Ireland, a related custom was guising: going from house to house in disguise and performing a song, a verse or a small act in return for food, coins or treats. Guising is recorded in Scotland from at least the sixteenth century, often around New Year as well as at Halloween. A Scottish account from 1895 describes Halloween guisers in disguise carrying lanterns made from scooped-out turnips, visiting homes and receiving cakes, fruit and money.

A stranger custom from Cork

Some old customs were odder than a simple mask. In 1853, a writer named William Hackett described a Halloween procession among young people in County Cork. A figure draped in a white sheet, carrying a horse's head and known as the White Mare, led a group of youths blowing cow horns from farm to farm, collecting contributions of milk, butter and other food. Customs like this show that disguise, noise and door-to-door collecting were part of the Irish Halloween season well before modern trick-or-treating.

Crossing the Atlantic

Irish and Scottish emigrants brought these customs to North America in large numbers during the nineteenth century. At first, Halloween in the United States and Canada was often a night for pranks and parties rather than organized door-to-door visiting. One of the earliest North American references to guising comes from Kingston, Ontario, where a newspaper reported children going "guising" around the neighborhood in 1911. The phrase "trick or treat" first appears in print in Ontario in 1917, and it spread through the United States in the 1930s and 1940s. Wartime sugar rationing slowed trick-or-treating for a few years, but by the early 1950s it was firmly established in American popular culture, showing up in Peanuts comics and a Disney cartoon.

Store-bought costumes

As the holiday grew, so did a costume industry. For many years most children wore homemade outfits made from sheets, old clothes and painted cardboard. Companies began selling boxed Halloween costumes in the 1930s. Ben Cooper, a New York company that started out making theatrical costumes, became one of the best-known makers, selling inexpensive outfits with printed smocks and plastic masks based on popular cartoon and comic characters. Mass-produced costumes made it easy for any child to become a favorite character, and dressing up spread from children to adults.

Why the ghost story stuck

The idea that costumes began as protection from the dead is appealing because it gives a modern party custom an ancient and spooky meaning. It may contain a kernel of truth. Folk belief in many places did connect late autumn with spirits and fairies, and some customs involved impersonating or appeasing them. But the specific claim that ancient Celts wore costumes at Samhain to fool ghosts is very hard to prove from the surviving evidence.

What really happened

Nobody can say for certain that Halloween costumes began as a way to hide from ghosts. That idea mostly comes from nineteenth-century folklorists, not from early Irish sources. What is well documented is a long line of door-to-door customs around Halloween and All Souls': souling for soul cakes, guising in disguise for treats and festive processions like the White Mare in Cork. Emigrants carried those customs to North America, where they grew into modern trick-or-treating and a whole industry of store-bought costumes.
