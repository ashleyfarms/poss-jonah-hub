One creature, many names

The Tatzelwurm is a creature of folklore from the Alps of Austria, Bavaria, Switzerland, Italy and France. Its names change from valley to valley. In Bavaria it was the Tatzelwurm, a name that later spread in Austria. The Swiss in the Bernese Alps called it the Stollenwurm or Stollwurm, often translated "tunnel worm" or "dragon of the mine tunnels." In parts of Austria it was the Bergstutz, "mountain stump," and in the French Alps it was the arassas. Similar stories also exist in southern Sweden.

What it looks like in the stories

Descriptions vary a lot. It is usually a stubby, lizard-like animal anywhere from 1 to 7 feet long, with a cat-like face (especially in Swiss accounts) and a body like a snake. Some versions give it four short legs, some only two front legs, and at least one gives it six. It is said to hiss, whistle or shriek, and in Austria and Bavaria its breath was said to be poisonous, even deadly. Poison breath is a classic trait of European dragons.

The old Swiss reports

In 1680 a Swiss writer, Johann Jacob Wagner, collected dragon sightings from Switzerland, and in 1723 the scholar Johann Jakob Scheuchzer published them with copperplate illustrations. Among them: a four-legged, cat-faced "mountain dragon" that Andreas Roduner said he met in 1660 on Mt. Wangersberg in Sarganserland. When it reared up on its back legs, he said, it was as tall as a man, with boar-like bristles down its back. Another story told of Hans and Thomas Tinner meeting a legless, cat-headed serpent seven feet long on the mountain of Frümsen; neighbors had been complaining that something was sucking their cows' udders, and the complaints stopped once the animal was killed. A 70-year-old man on Mt. Kamor said a dragon's breath gave him a headache and dizziness.

The farmer story in the video, from 1779, is the famous Hans Fuchs tale: he said he saw two of the creatures, ran home in terror and died soon after, telling his family what he had seen.

What naturalists said

In 1887 the naturalist Karl Wilhelm von Dalla Torre, writing a history of Alpine dragons, concluded that all of these creatures could be explained as ordinary lizards or snakes, even if that left the cat faces unexplained. Other writers kept collecting "new" Tatzelwurm stories into the 1890s. Scheuchzer has often been mocked for believing in dragons, though historians note that scholars of his time tried to keep an open mind about nature.

The Alps do have real candidates: adders and other snakes, the fire salamander with its bold black-and-yellow skin, and large lizards. An animal seen for a second among rocks, by someone who already knows the dragon stories, can grow a cat face pretty quickly. So: real dragon, or a salamander with good publicity? You decide.
