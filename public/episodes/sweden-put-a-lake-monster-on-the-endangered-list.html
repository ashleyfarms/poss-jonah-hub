Well, That's Weird: Cryptids

Sweden Put a Lake Monster on the Endangered List

Cryptids are creatures people report but science has never confirmed. In each episode Poss Jonah tells where the legend comes from, what people say they saw, and what a sensible explanation might be, then lets you decide.

The story

In Sweden, a lake monster was once protected by law as an endangered species, and its nest too. Meet Storsjöodjuret, the Great Lake Monster, said to live in Lake Storsjön, smack in the middle of Sweden. A manuscript from 1635 tells of a lake serpent, bound to the bottom by a rune master, who carved his spell into a runestone.

Later folk tales said two trolls brewed it up in a cauldron, with a black serpent body and a head like a cat. In 1894, locals formed a company to catch it, backed by King Oscar the Second. But they never caught it. Hundreds of sightings have been reported since the 1800s. And then in 1986, the county declared it endangered, protecting the monster, its young and its nest, till the law was revoked in 2005.

But so what's down there? An 1899 naturalist figured it was some water animal nobody had named yet. And nobody's caught one. But you decide. I'm Poss Jonah.

Where the legend starts

Storsjöodjuret means "the Great-Lake Monster" in Swedish. The lake in question, Storsjön, sits in the province of Jämtland in the middle of Sweden and is about 90 meters (roughly 300 feet) deep at its deepest point. The only city on its shore is Östersund, and the island of Frösön sits out in the water just across from town.

The oldest written mention we know of comes from a manuscript by Morten Pedersen Herdal dated 1635. It describes a lake serpent (a sjöorm) that a legendary rune master, Kettil Runske, bound to the bottom of the lake by carving a spell into the Frösö Runestone on the island. A 1685 account by Anders Plantin adds that the serpent's head was said to lie under the stone, with its body stretching across the lake to villages on the far shore, and that strange things happened in the water whenever the stone was knocked down.

Here is the fun twist. The Frösö Runestone is real, and it does have a big carved serpent on it. But the runic text itself says nothing about a monster or about Kettil Runske. It records a man named Austmaðr, son of Guðfastr, who had Jämtland christened and built a bridge. The stone really has been broken in two at some point, which may be part of why the story stuck to it.

The troll version

Later folk tales told in Jämtland give the monster a different origin. Two trolls, named Jata and Kata in the legend, stood on the shore stirring their cauldrons for days, weeks and months. At last there was a groan like a sick child, a bang like thunder, and out jumped a creature with a black serpent body and a cat-like head. It slid into the lake and kept growing until, in the story, it could wrap all the way around Frösön and bite its own tail. A hero bound it with a carved spell, and the tale warns that it could get loose again if anyone ever reads the runes.

What people say they have seen

Hundreds of sightings have been recorded since the 1800s. In July 1857, newspapers reported that workers at an iron mill saw a creature with a black, gleaming head about the size of a large cat's. In 1899 a naturalist named Peter Olsson published a booklet collecting 22 eyewitness accounts. Lengths in those reports ranged from about 3.5 to 14 meters (11 to 46 feet). Some witnesses described a snake-like body and long neck, some described a dog-like head with what might be ears or fins, and some described humps or wave-like bends along its back.

Olsson's conclusion was a careful one. He thought the most likely answer was some water-dwelling mammal that science had not identified yet. Notice what he did not say: he did not claim it was a dragon, a troll creation, or a dinosaur.

The 1894 hunt and the protection law

Interest peaked in the 1890s. In 1894 local people organized a company to catch the monster, and it even had the backing of King Oscar II. They built traps and tried for years, but they never caught anything. The failed hunt was even mocked in a satirical cartoon in the Swedish humor magazine Strix.

The strangest chapter came in 1986, the year Östersund celebrated its 200th anniversary. The Jämtland county administrative board declared Storsjöodjuret an endangered species, and the monster, its offspring and its nest were protected by law. The protection stayed on the books until 2005, when it was revoked after criticism led by Sweden's Parliamentary Ombudsman. In 2008 a film crew said infrared cameras had picked up a warm mass in the lake, but that footage did not settle anything.

So what could it be?

Big, deep, cold lakes are good at producing monster stories. Floating logs, boat wakes that cross and stand up as a hump, swimming animals seen low against the water, and odd waves all look strange from a distance, especially when people already expect a monster. None of that proves there is nothing in Storsjön, and none of the sightings proves there is something. What we can say for sure is that the legend is centuries old, the runestone is real, the 1894 hunt really happened, and for nineteen years a Swedish county gave a monster legal protection.
