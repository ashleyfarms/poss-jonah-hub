D.B. Cooper: He Jumped Out of a Plane With $200,000

Odd, non-graphic true-crime stories and courtroom curiosities.

The story

A quiet passenger

On 24 November 1971, the day before Thanksgiving, a man in a dark business suit, white shirt and thin black tie walked up to the Northwest Orient Airlines counter at Portland International Airport in Oregon. He paid cash for a one-way ticket on Flight 305, a short afternoon hop north to Seattle-Tacoma International Airport. On the ticket he gave his name as Dan Cooper.

Witnesses later described him as a calm, polite white man in his mid-40s with dark hair. He carried a black attaché case and took a seat in the last row of the Boeing 727. Flight 305 left Portland on schedule a little before 3 p.m., with 36 passengers and a crew of six. After takeoff, Cooper ordered a bourbon drink and lit a cigarette, which passengers were allowed to do on planes in those days.

The note

Soon after takeoff, Cooper handed a note to a flight attendant, Florence Schaffner. She assumed it was a phone number from a passenger flirting with her and tucked it away unread. Cooper leaned over and told her she had better look at it: he had a bomb. When she sat beside him, he opened his case enough to show her what looked like red cylinders, wires and a battery.

His demands were relayed to the cockpit and then to the airline: $200,000 in cash, in $20 bills, and four parachutes, delivered when the plane landed in Seattle. Flight attendant Tina Mucklow sat with him for most of the ordeal and passed messages between him and the crew. She later said he seemed to know the area. Looking out the window, he remarked that it looked like Tacoma below, and he correctly noted that McChord Air Force Base was only about a 20-minute drive from the Seattle airport.

Seattle

The 727 circled over Puget Sound for about two hours while the FBI, Seattle police and the airline gathered the money and parachutes. Most of the passengers were told only that there was a mechanical delay. The airline's president agreed to pay the ransom. The FBI made sure the bills were photographed on microfilm first, so that every serial number was recorded.

At 5:46 p.m. Pacific time the plane landed at Seattle-Tacoma. Following Cooper's instructions, Mucklow went out to collect the bag of cash, about 10,000 bills, and then made several trips for the parachutes. Cooper released all 36 passengers and two of the flight attendants. He kept the pilots, the flight engineer and Mucklow aboard.

Into the night

Cooper told the crew to fly to Mexico City, with a refueling stop in Reno, Nevada. He gave specific instructions: fly low and slow, with the wing flaps partly lowered and the landing gear down. The plane took off from Seattle around 7:40 p.m. Two Air Force fighter jets and a trainer aircraft followed at a distance, but in the dark and the clouds they never saw anything.

Cooper sent Mucklow to the cockpit and told the crew to stay there. Then he lowered the rear airstair, a set of steps that folded down from the tail of the 727. At about 8:13 p.m., somewhere over the rugged, forested country of southwest Washington, the crew felt the tail of the plane bump upward, as if a weight had suddenly come off the stairs. When the 727 landed in Reno just after 11 p.m. with the stairs still down, Cooper was gone, along with the money.

The search

FBI agents found few clues aboard. They collected dozens of fingerprints that were never matched to anyone. They also found two of the four parachutes and Cooper's black clip-on tie, still fitted with a mother-of-pearl tie clip, left behind at his seat.

Using the crew's timing and the plane's route, investigators worked out roughly where he might have jumped and searched large areas of Clark and Cowlitz counties in Washington, near the Lewis River and Lake Merwin. In 1972, hundreds of soldiers, agents and volunteers combed the forests on foot. A submarine even searched the depths of Lake Merwin. Nothing turned up: no parachute, no body, no money. The serial numbers were distributed to banks, casinos and businesses around the world, and newspapers printed them and offered rewards, but none of the bills appeared in circulation.

Along the way, a wire-service reporter mixed up the hijacker with a suspect named D. B. Cooper, who was quickly cleared. The wrong name stuck, and the case has been known as the D. B. Cooper hijacking ever since.

The money on the riverbank

On 10 February 1980, an eight-year-old boy named Brian Ingram was on a family outing along the Columbia River at a beach called Tena (or Tina) Bar, downstream from Vancouver, Washington. While smoothing out sand for a campfire, he uncovered three bundles of decaying $20 bills. The serial numbers matched Cooper's ransom. In total, about $5,800 was recovered.

The discovery raised as many questions as it answered. Scientists and investigators have argued for decades about how the bundles got there: whether they washed down a river, were moved by dredging work or were buried by someone. The location did not fit neatly with the FBI's original idea of where Cooper landed. In 1986, after a legal dispute, the recovered money was split between Ingram and the airline's insurer, with the FBI keeping some bills as evidence.

Science and suspects

Over the years, the FBI looked at hundreds of possible suspects, and amateur investigators have proposed many more. Several men were investigated closely, and some families have claimed a relative was the hijacker, but no one has ever been proven to be Cooper. In 2001, the FBI took samples from the tie and later developed a partial DNA profile, but the agency noted that it could not be sure the DNA came from Cooper, and it never produced a match. Independent researchers have also examined tiny particles found on the tie, hoping to trace where its owner worked.

Many experts, including some at the FBI, believed Cooper was unlikely to have survived. He jumped at night, in bad weather, over rough terrain, wearing a business suit and loafers, without a helmet or proper gear. Others argue that someone who knew enough to ask for specific flight settings may have had real parachuting experience.

Case suspended

On 8 July 2016, the FBI announced that it was suspending active investigation of the Cooper case so it could focus its resources on other priorities. The agency said it would still consider physical evidence related to the parachutes or the money if any were found.

What really happened

A man calling himself Dan Cooper hijacked a passenger jet with a threat of a bomb, collected $200,000 and parachuted out of the back of the plane into the night over southwest Washington. Apart from a few thousand dollars found on a Columbia River beach in 1980, the money was never recovered. More than fifty years later, nobody knows who he was or what happened to him. The FBI has described it as the only unsolved air piracy in American aviation history.
