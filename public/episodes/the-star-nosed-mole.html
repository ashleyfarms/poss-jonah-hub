Well, That's Weird: Animals

The Star-Nosed Mole

Real animals that sound made up. Every claim in these episodes is checked against published science or museum sources.

The story

A nose like no other

The star-nosed mole (Condylura cristata) looks like something invented for a cartoon. It is a small, dark mole about the size of a hamster, with velvety black fur, big shovel-like front paws and a long, thick tail. The striking part is its face. Ringing the tip of its snout are 22 pink, fleshy rays, arranged in a starburst about a centimeter across, 11 on each side. When the mole is hunting, the rays move constantly, fluttering over everything in front of it.

It is the only mole in the world with a nose like this, and scientists have spent decades working out what it does. The answer turns out to be remarkable: the star is one of the most sensitive touch organs known in any mammal, and it helps make the star-nosed mole the fastest eater among mammals.

Where it lives

Star-nosed moles live in eastern North America, from southeastern Canada and the Great Lakes region down through the northeastern United States, with populations extending south along the Atlantic coast and the Appalachians. Unlike many moles, which prefer dry soil, the star-nosed mole loves wet places: marshes, swamps, bogs, the banks of streams and ponds, and soggy meadows.

It digs shallow tunnels in moist soil, and those tunnels often open directly into water. The star-nosed mole is a good swimmer and forages along the bottoms of streams and ponds as well as underground. It stays active through the winter and has been seen tunneling through snow and swimming in streams under the ice.

A tactile eye

Like other moles, the star-nosed mole has tiny eyes and very poor vision, which is not much use in dark tunnels and murky water. Instead, it explores the world by touch. The surface of its star is covered with thousands of tiny, dome-shaped sensory structures called Eimer's organs, named after the German zoologist Theodor Eimer, who first described them in the European mole in 1872. The star-nosed mole has more than 25,000 of these organs packed onto its star, connected to more than 100,000 nerve fibers. That is several times the number of touch fibers in the human hand, crowded onto an area smaller than a fingertip.

Neuroscientist Kenneth Catania of Vanderbilt University, who has studied the species for many years, has compared the star to an eye that works by touch. The outer rays sweep across the ground, quickly scanning a wide area. When they touch something interesting, the mole shifts its star so that the smallest pair of rays, the 11th pair at the bottom center, makes contact. That pair has the highest density of nerve endings and acts like the fovea of a human eye, the small central area of the retina that sees in the sharpest detail. In the mole's brain, the area devoted to those two small rays is much larger than their size alone would suggest, just as the human brain gives extra space to the center of vision.

The rays themselves have no bones or muscles of their own for grabbing. They are not used to catch food, only to feel for it.

The fastest eater among mammals

In 2005, Catania and his colleague Fiona Remple published a study in the journal Nature about how quickly star-nosed moles find and eat their food. Using high-speed video, they found that a mole could detect a small prey item, decide it was edible and eat it in an average of about 230 milliseconds, less than a quarter of a second. The fastest examples took only about 120 milliseconds. That speed earned the star-nosed mole recognition as the fastest-eating mammal.

The researchers noted that this is close to the limit of how fast nerve signals can travel from the star to the brain and back to the muscles, so the mole's brain is making decisions about as fast as its nervous system allows. The speed makes sense for a small, high-energy animal that hunts very small prey, such as worms, insect larvae and tiny aquatic creatures. When each item is small, being able to find and eat many of them quickly makes a big difference.

Smelling underwater

Catania's research uncovered another surprise. Mammals usually cannot smell underwater, since breathing in water would mean inhaling it. Star-nosed moles get around this by blowing small air bubbles out of their nostrils onto objects or scent trails and then sniffing the bubbles back in, catching scent molecules in the process. High-speed video showed the moles doing this as they followed trails underwater. The water shrew does something similar.

Family life

Star-nosed moles mate in late winter or early spring. A female usually has one litter a year of about four or five young, born in late spring or early summer in a nest of dry plant material. The babies are born hairless and tiny, about 5 centimeters (2 inches) long, but they grow quickly and leave the nest within about a month. Their main predators include owls, hawks, foxes, skunks, weasels and large fish and frogs that catch them while swimming.

Not in danger

Despite its odd appearance and specialized lifestyle, the star-nosed mole is not considered threatened. The International Union for Conservation of Nature lists it as a species of least concern, because it has a wide range and is fairly common where suitable wet habitat remains. Like many wetland animals, it depends on marshes and stream banks being protected from draining and development.

What really happened

The star-nosed mole's strange nose is not a decoration. It is a super-sensitive touch organ with more than 25,000 tiny sensors that lets a nearly blind animal explore the world in fine detail. Working like a touch-based eye, it helps the mole find and eat small prey in a fraction of a second, which researchers have measured as the fastest eating of any mammal. Nature's oddest nose turns out to be one of its finest tools.
