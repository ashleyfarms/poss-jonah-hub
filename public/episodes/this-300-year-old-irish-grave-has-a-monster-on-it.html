Well, That's Weird: Cryptids

This 300-Year-Old Irish Grave Has a Monster on It

Cryptids are creatures people report but science has never confirmed. In each episode Poss Jonah tells where the legend comes from, what people say they saw, and what a sensible explanation might be, then lets you decide.

The story

Meet the Dobhar-chú, The water hound. He's half a dog. He's half an otter. He's 10 to 15 feet long with white fur, black ear tips, and a black cross on his back. The legend goes, in 1722, Grace Connolly went down to Glenade Lough to wash clothes. And never came home. Her husband, Terrence, found the beast asleep beside her and killed it with a dagger.

As it died, it let out a whistling cry, and its mate rose from the lake and chased him, but he killed that one too. That was a bad old boy, wasn't it? Her headstone, they say, shows a dog-like beast with a tufted tail. In 2003, an artist claimed he saw a big dark one off of Omey Island and it had orange flipper feet.

I'm wondering if that fella wasn't trying some of Ireland's best, yeah, you know what I mean? But here's the catch. Dobhar-chú is one of the regular Irish words for otter, so a monster or the biggest otter story ever told. Well, you decide. Hi, I'm Poss Jonah. Well, That's Weird.

What the Dobhar-chú is

The Dobhar-chú (pronounced roughly "DOO-ar-koo") is a creature of Irish and Scottish folklore. The name means "water hound" or "water dog," and it is also called the King Otter. Stories describe something like a giant otter, about five times the normal size at 10 to 15 feet long, with a white pelt, black tips on its ears, and a dark mark like a cross on its back. Some versions make the fur darker because of the murky water it lives in, and some say its fur has protective powers.

Here is the part that makes language lovers grin. Dobharchú is one of the two most common Irish words for an ordinary otter. Dobhar is an old word for water that survives in place names, and cú means hound, the same word found in the name of the hero Cúchulainn.

The Glenade story

The most famous tale is set in 1722 at Glenade Lough in County Leitrim. A woman named Grace McGloighlin, known locally by her maiden name, Grace Connolly, lived in the townland of Creevelea at the northwest corner of the lake. She went down to the water one morning to wash clothes. One version says her husband, Terrence, heard her scream; another says he went looking when she did not come home. Both say he found her dead with the creature asleep on top of her. He ran home for a dagger and killed it. As it died, it gave a whistling cry, and a second Dobhar-chú rose from the lake and chased him. After a long fight, he killed that one too.

The headstone

The story would be just a story if not for a real grave marker. In Conwall Cemetery (Congbháil) in Glenade there is a sandstone slab, about 4.6 feet by 1.1 feet, carved in relief with a lying-down animal that looks like a dog with a tufted tail. Local tradition says it marks Grace's grave and shows the beast that killed her. People still visit it today. The carving is real; what it is meant to show is the part people argue about.

Modern sightings

Sightings have been claimed over the years. The best known recent one is from 2003, when Irish artist Sean Corcoran and his wife said they saw a large, dark creature off Omey Island in Connemara, County Galway. He said it swam fast, gave a haunting screech, and had orange, flipper-like feet.

Big otter, or something else?

Eurasian otters live all over Ireland and are much bigger than many people expect, especially an old male seen briefly in the water. They whistle and squeal, which lines up nicely with the "whistling cry" in the legend. Seals also come into bays and river mouths and can look dog-headed. On the other hand, a 10-to-15-foot otter would be something no biologist has ever recorded. The honest answer is that the Dobhar-chú is a strong piece of oral tradition, backed by a real gravestone, with no physical evidence of a giant animal. You decide.
