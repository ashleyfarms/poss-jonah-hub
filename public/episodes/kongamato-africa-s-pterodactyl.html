Well, That's Weird: Cryptids

Kongamato (Africa's "pterodactyl")

Cryptids are creatures people report but science has never confirmed. In each episode Poss Jonah tells where the legend comes from, what people say they saw, and what a sensible explanation might be, then lets you decide.

The story

In 1923, folks near a swamp in Africa were shown a picture of a flying reptile from the dinosaur days and they said, that's it. meet the kongamato, the "overwhelmer of boats," said to live in the Jiundu Swamp in Western Zambia. Now, it's described as featherless with reddish skin, a toothy beak, and wings four to seven feet across.

Legend says it dives into the water and flips canoes from below. One writer has said that no one ever saw it close and lived to tell a tale. In 1957, a man came into a hospital with bad chest wounds. He said a big bird attacked him in the swamp and we ain't talking Sesame Street.

And when doctors asked him to draw it, he reportedly sketched a pterosaur. But skeptics say pterosaurs couldn't fold their wings to dive like that and point to big strange looking birds like the shoebill stork. And others guessed a giant bat. Now you tell me how a bat is going to flip a boat and stab a dude in the chest.

It ain't happening. This is a dinosaur. You hear me? But you decide.

Where the name comes from

The kongamato is a creature reported from the Jiundu Swamp in the Mwinilunga district of western Zambia, with related stories from Angola and the Democratic Republic of the Congo. The name is usually translated "overwhelmer of boats," because the legend says it attacks canoes by diving into the water and flipping them from below. Descriptions give it a wingspan of about 4 to 7 feet (1.2 to 2.1 meters), no feathers, smooth reddish skin, and a beak full of teeth. Some accounts add a tail.

The 1923 book

The story reached English readers through Frank Melland's 1923 book In Witch-Bound Africa. Melland wrote that people of the Kaonde nation, when shown a picture of a pterosaur, said it looked just like the kongamato. He also wrote the line that made the creature famous: details were hard to pin down because "no one ever saw a kongamato close and lived to tell the tale." Local tradition apparently treated even seeing it as dangerous.

A couple of years later, journalist G. Ward Price wrote that he and the future King Edward VIII heard an eyewitness account in Southern Rhodesia from a man who had been hurt in a feared swamp and who picked out a pterosaur drawing as the thing that attacked him.

Later reports

In 1932, naturalist Gerald Russell and writer Ivan T. Sanderson said they saw a large flying animal together in Cameroon; Sanderson thought it was a kind of hammerhead bat. An engineer, J.P.F. Brown, reported a sighting in 1956, and a man identified as D. Gregor and his wife also claimed to have seen flying reptiles. In 1957, as the episode says, a man with serious chest injuries came to a hospital and said a large bird had attacked him in the Bangweulu Swamps. When doctors asked him to draw it, he reportedly sketched a pterosaur. Near the end of the 1950s a photograph was said to show an unknown animal in the Zambezi Valley, but analysis suggested it was most likely a shoebill or saddle-billed stork.

What skeptics say

Pterosaurs disappear from the fossil record about 66 million years ago, so a living one would be a gap of a staggering size. Skeptics also point out that what we know about how pterosaurs folded their wings makes it hard to picture one diving into water and flipping a canoe.

There are real animals in that part of Africa that look prehistoric. The shoebill is a huge gray bird with a massive bill. The saddle-billed stork stands as tall as a person and has a long, colorful bill. Marabou storks are big and bald-headed. The writer Rick Emmer suggested that storks defending their nests might explain the boat-attack stories. A museum director in Southern Rhodesia, Reay Smithers, suggested the shoebill in 1957, and zoologist Maurice Burton agreed in the early 1960s. Several cryptozoologists themselves, including Sanderson and Bernard Heuvelmans, leaned toward a big bat. In 1928 a paleontology professor from Uppsala University, Carl Wiman, even suggested the legend could have grown out of local people helping dig up real pterosaur fossils in Tanzania before World War I.

Our take

What we can document is a story first written down in 1923, a handful of later reports, and some genuinely strange-looking birds living in the same swamps. Poss Jonah still says it's a dinosaur, but the evidence says storks are the safer bet. You decide.
