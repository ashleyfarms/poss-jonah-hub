The Glass Frog That Hides Its Blood

Real animals that sound made up. Every claim in these episodes is checked against published science or museum sources.

The story

When this little frog sleeps, it hides nearly all its blood in its liver to turn see-through. Meet the glassfrog of Central America. At rest, its muscles and skin go clear. You can see bones, eyes, and organs. In 2022, Duke researchers used special imaging and found resting glassfrogs pull about 90% of their red blood cells out of circulation.

They pack those cells into the liver, which has a mirror-like lining that helps hide the red. Now when the frog wakes up and gets moving, those cells flow right back into the blood. And somehow they don't form a dangerous clot. Scientists are still trying to figure that part out. So, what do we really got?

We got a tiny leaf frog with a blood-hiding trick as reported in the journal Science. I wish I had this trick. It would come in handy when I'm out there turkey hunting. I'm Poss Jonah.

What a glass frog is

Glass frogs are a family of small tree frogs called the Centrolenidae, found in rainforests from southern Mexico through Central America and into South America, with the greatest variety in the Andes. Most are lime green on top. What gives them their name is the underside: in many species the belly skin is so clear that you can see the heart, liver and gut right through it. The family was named in 1945, and dozens of species have been described since, many of them from Colombia and Ecuador.

The 2022 discovery

For a long time nobody understood how the frogs could look so see-through when red blood is flowing through every part of a living body. In December 2022, a team led by researchers at Duke University and the American Museum of Natural History published the answer in the journal Science. They studied a glass frog species called Hyalinobatrachium fleischmanni, which sleeps upside down on the underside of leaves during the day.

Using a technique called photoacoustic imaging, which uses light and sound to map where red blood cells are without disturbing the animal, the team found that a sleeping frog pulls about 89 percent of its red blood cells out of circulation and packs them into its liver. The liver is covered with a reflective, mirror-like layer, so the stored blood does not show. That makes the frog two to three times more transparent while it rests. When the frog wakes up and starts moving, the cells flow back out into the body and the frog becomes visibly less clear.

Why it is a medical puzzle

In people, packing that much blood together would be a recipe for a dangerous clot. The frogs do it every day and are fine. That is why the work interested medical researchers as well as biologists: if scientists can figure out how the frogs avoid clotting, it could someday help with blood-clot research. As of now, the frogs are keeping that secret.

Why see-through helps

A frog sleeping on top of a green leaf is easy for a bird or snake to spot. A see-through frog lets the leaf color show through its body, and its outline fades. Researchers have also found that some glass frogs reflect near-infrared light the way leaves do, which may help them blend in for predators that see differently than we do.

Devoted dads

Glass frogs lay their eggs on leaves hanging over streams. In many species the male guards the eggs for days or even weeks, keeping them moist and fending off insects. When the tadpoles hatch, they drop into the stream below, and grow up in the water and leaf litter before climbing back into the trees as adults. A tiny frog with a body you can see through, a blood-hiding trick, and a father who stands guard: that is about as "well, that's weird" as nature gets.
