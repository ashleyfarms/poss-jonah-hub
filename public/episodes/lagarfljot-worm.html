Well, That's Weird: Cryptids

Lagarfljót Worm

Cryptids are creatures people report but science has never confirmed. In each episode Poss Jonah tells where the legend comes from, what people say they saw, and what a sensible explanation might be, then lets you decide.

The story

A serpent in the lake

In the east of Iceland, near the town of Egilsstaðir, a long, narrow body of water called Lagarfljót stretches for more than 20 kilometers through a glacier-fed valley. The lake is fed by meltwater carrying fine silt, which makes the water murky and hard to see into. For more than six centuries, people living nearby have told stories of something large moving under its surface: the Lagarfljót Worm, or Lagarfljótsormurinn in Icelandic.

In Old Norse and Icelandic, "worm" (ormur) can mean a serpent or dragon, not a small crawling creature. The Lagarfljót Worm is usually described as a long, many-humped serpent, sometimes said to be longer than a bus, around 12 meters (40 feet) or more. Some reports describe humps rising out of the water. Others claim to have seen it coiled on the shore or slithering into the trees.

Sightings through the centuries

The earliest record usually tied to the creature comes from 1345. Icelandic annals of that year describe a "wonderful thing" or marvel seen in Lagarfljót. The text does not actually call it a serpent. It describes objects that looked like islands or humps in the water, separated by long distances, with no head or tail visible. Later writers came to treat the entry as the first sighting of the Worm, and more strange appearances were recorded in the annals over the following centuries.

The serpent soon found its way onto maps. A map of Iceland attributed to Bishop Guðbrandur Þorláksson and engraved by the famous cartographer Abraham Ortelius in 1585 includes a Latin note beside Lagarfljót. It says, in translation, that a serpent of unusual size appears in the lake, threatens the inhabitants and shows itself when something memorable is about to happen. A Mercator map from 1595 also notes a giant serpent there.

Seventeenth-century Icelandic writers described the creature as well, debating how many humps it had and blaming it for floods and for shaking the ground. A one-humped monster was reported at the lake in 1749 and 1750. Sightings continued into modern times. In 1963 the head of Iceland's forestry service, Sigurður Blöndal, reported seeing something unusual in the water, and in 1998 a teacher and students at a local school reported a sighting.

The girl, the gold and the slug

The best-known folk tale about the creature was published by the Icelandic folklorist Jón Árnason in 1862, from a story collected in the region in 1845. In it, a girl is given a gold ring by her mother, who tells her that the way to make gold grow is to place it under a "heath-worm," a small creature usually understood to be a black slug. The girl puts the ring under a slug in the top of her linen chest.

A few days later she finds that the slug has grown so large it has broken the chest open. Terrified, she throws it and the gold into the lake. There the creature keeps growing into a giant serpent that spits poison and kills people and animals. In the tale, two men from Finland are hired to destroy it, but they can only tie its head and tail to the bottom of the lake, because an even larger dragon lurks beneath it. From then on, whenever the serpent's humps appeared above the water, people saw it as a sign of a hard year or failed hay crops.

Folklorists have pointed out that the tale shares features with older Norse stories, such as dragons that grow along with the gold they guard. Similar "slug and gold" stories are attached to several other lakes and rivers in Iceland.

The broken cable

One of the most quoted modern stories comes from 1983. Workers laying a telephone cable across the lake reported measuring a large, shifting mass near the eastern shore during preliminary depth soundings. When they later pulled up the cable, it had been badly damaged. One worker said the specially engineered cable, designed not to kink, had been wound up in several places and torn in 22 places. He joked that they must have dragged it across the belly of the beast. There is no clear explanation for the damage, and skeptics note that ice, rocks, currents and heavy objects on the lakebed can all damage cables.

The 2012 video

In February 2012, Iceland's national broadcaster, RÚV, aired a video shot by a local farmer that appeared to show something long and dark undulating through partly frozen water in a river feeding the lake. The clip spread around the world.

Not everyone was convinced. A frame-by-frame analysis by Finland-based researcher Miisa McKeown found that the object never actually moved forward. It stayed in roughly the same place while its shape rippled, creating an illusion of swimming. A likely explanation, she suggested, was something flimsy and lifeless, such as a frozen fishing net or a piece of fabric, being tugged by the fast current.

Even so, later in 2012 a panel set up by the local municipality, which had offered a reward for proof of the Worm, voted seven to six that the video was genuine and paid the prize to the man who filmed it. Critics saw the vote as a way to attract tourists. In August 2014, a local "truth commission" reported that its members were divided about the video but saw no reason to doubt that the creature existed.

What could explain it?

Scientists have offered several possible explanations for sightings over the years. The lake's murky, silty water makes it hard to judge the size or shape of anything near the surface. Floating logs, mats of plant material, waves, unusual currents and changing light could all look like humps. Gases released from the lakebed could make the water bubble or bulge. Large fish or seals could create surprising wakes. The cold, nutrient-poor waters of Lagarfljót would make it very difficult for a huge animal to find enough food, and no physical remains of any such creature have ever been found.

A monster for tourism

Whatever lives in Lagarfljót, the Worm has become an important part of local culture and tourism. A sightseeing boat named Lagarfljótsormurinn began operating on the lake in 1999, and local cultural institutions work to preserve the stories surrounding it. Visitors come hoping for a glimpse, and souvenir shops sell Worm-themed gifts.

What really happened

People have reported strange things in Lagarfljót for more than six hundred years, from a mysterious marvel in the 1345 annals to a serpent on a 1585 map to a viral video in 2012. A folk tale explains it as a slug that grew huge on gold. Scientists suspect murky water, currents, floating debris and optical illusions, and frame-by-frame analysis of the famous video suggests a lifeless object in the current. No one has proven the Worm exists, but the legend is very much alive. You decide.
