Sovereign Citizens Pt 1: The Secret Twin Theory

Odd, non-graphic true-crime stories and courtroom curiosities.

The story

The Oregon Historical Society says it began in 1969 with a Portland man and an idea. The county sheriff outranks everybody. Scholars say it came wrapped in racist and anti-Semitic ideas. Later, promoters sold redemption kits. Your name in capital letters is a strawman with a secret treasury account. Well, mine must be misplaced. But how many are dumb enough to believe it? Nobody's got an official count, but a watchdog group says as many as 350 to 400,000 fall for this. But those are just estimates, and they're disputed. It runs from the curious and the ticket dodgers to folks who bought paperwork kits and a small fringe the FBI calls violent extremists.

An Australian parliamentary paper says most aren't violent. That paper also says no court in the world has accepted these arguments. Best advice? Just pay the ticket.

Where the movement came from

The sovereign citizen idea grew out of a group called the Posse Comitatus, Latin for "force of the county." The Oregon Historical Society traces it to Portland around 1969. Its core belief was that the county sheriff is the highest legitimate authority in the land and that federal and state governments had overstepped. Historians and civil-rights researchers have documented that the early movement was tied up with racist and antisemitic beliefs. Over the following decades, its legal-sounding arguments spread far beyond that original group, and many people who repeat them today have never heard of where they started.

The "secret twin" or strawman theory

The strawman theory took shape around 1999 and 2000, growing out of an older "redemption" scheme promoted by a North Dakota man named Roger Elvick. It claims that when the government issues a birth certificate, it creates a separate legal person, a "strawman," whose name is spelled in ALL CAPITAL LETTERS on official papers. According to the theory, the government used these strawmen as collateral, and each one has a secret account at the U.S. Treasury worth a large sum of money. The flesh-and-blood person, supposedly, can file the right forms to "redeem" the strawman and draw on that account to pay debts or taxes. This is often called "redemption" theory.

Promoters sold seminars and paperwork kits to teach the method. People who tried it filed fake money orders, bogus liens and invented financial instruments. None of it works. There is no secret account, and capital letters on a form are simply a typesetting convention. Courts have rejected these arguments every time they have been raised, and people have been prosecuted for passing the fake financial documents and for filing false liens against public officials.

How many people believe it

Nobody has an official count. Advocacy groups and researchers have published estimates in the hundreds of thousands in the United States, but those are estimates and are disputed. The people involved range widely: some are curious, some are trying to get out of a traffic ticket or a debt, some bought into a paperwork kit during a hard time, and a small fringe are violent. The FBI has described sovereign citizen extremists as a domestic terrorism threat and has warned police about the danger in traffic stops.

What courts say

Judges in several countries have now written long decisions on these arguments. The best known is a 2012 Canadian ruling, Meads v. Meads, from a provincial Court of Queen's Bench, where Associate Chief Justice John Rooke catalogued what he called "Organized Pseudolegal Commercial Arguments" and explained why each one fails. In the United States, the IRS keeps a public document called "The Truth About Frivolous Tax Arguments," which lists the strawman and redemption claims and warns that filing them can bring a $5,000 penalty. Courts in Australia, New Zealand, the United Kingdom and elsewhere have reached the same result.

The practical takeaway

If someone offers to sell you a way to unlock your "strawman account," stop. It is a scam, and the person who loses money or ends up in court is the one who believes it. If you have a real legal problem, like debt, taxes or a ticket, a licensed attorney, a legal aid office or a nonprofit credit counselor can help for far less than a seminar costs. Or, in the words of this episode, just pay the ticket.
