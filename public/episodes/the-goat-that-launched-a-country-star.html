Well, That's Weird: History

The Goat That Launched a Country Star

True, odd moments from history, told short and checked against at least two sources.

The story

A kid from Sabinal

Juan Raoul Davis "Johnny" Rodriguez was born on 10 December 1951 in Sabinal, a small town in Uvalde County, Texas, west of San Antonio. He grew up in a Mexican American family of ten children, packed into a four-room house. By most accounts he was a good student, an altar boy and the captain of his junior high football team. He also loved music and picked up the guitar young.

His teenage years took a hard turn. When he was 16, his father died of cancer, and the next year his older brother Andres was killed in a car accident. Rodriguez later spoke about how those losses sent him off course. In 1969, at 18, he landed in jail.

The goat story

For decades, the most famous detail of Johnny Rodriguez's life was a goat. As the story was usually told, he and some friends were caught after stealing a goat and barbecuing it, and Rodriguez took the blame. It was a good story, it fit the image of a rowdy young outlaw country singer, and it followed him for the rest of his career. Interviewers asked about it, fans joked about it at shows, and it turned up again in the obituaries when he died.

Whether there was really a goat is less certain. Joaquin Jackson, the Texas Ranger who plays a key role in the story, later said Rodriguez was in jail simply because of an unpaid fine. Every version agrees on what happened next, though: in that cell, Rodriguez sang.

The Texas Ranger who listened

H. Joaquin Jackson was a Texas Ranger assigned to a large stretch of the Texas-Mexico border from 1966 to 1993. He would later become something of a legend himself, partly thanks to a 1994 Texas Monthly cover that made him the face of the modern Rangers. In 1969, he heard the young prisoner singing and was impressed by his voice.

Jackson told a friend about him: James "Happy" Shahan, a rancher and promoter near Brackettville, Texas. Shahan owned Alamo Village, a full-scale frontier town and replica of the Alamo built on his ranch for John Wayne's 1960 film The Alamo. After the movie, the set became a tourist attraction and a location for other westerns, and Shahan put on live entertainment for visitors. On Jackson's word, Shahan hired Rodriguez to sing.

From Alamo Village to Nashville

Rodriguez performed at Alamo Village, singing and playing for tourists. In 1971, two established country stars, Tom T. Hall and Bobby Bare, came through and heard him. Both encouraged him to try his luck in Nashville.

He did. The usual telling says he arrived in Nashville with a guitar and $14 in his pocket. Tom T. Hall gave him work fronting his band and writing songs. Within a year, Hall took him to Mercury Records, where Rodriguez auditioned and was offered a recording contract.

A string of hits

His first single, "Pass Me By (If You're Only Passing Through)," came out in 1972 and reached number 9 on Billboard's country chart. That same year, the Academy of Country Music named him its most promising male vocalist. In 1973 he scored his first number-one country hit, "You Always Come Back (To Hurting Me)," followed by another, "Ridin' My Thumb to Mexico," which he wrote himself. His debut album topped the country albums chart.

The hits kept coming through the 1970s, including three number-one singles in 1975 alone. Over his career he reached number one on the country chart six times. He often sang a verse or chorus in Spanish, and he is widely remembered as the first major Mexican American star in mainstream country music, opening a door for later Latino artists in Nashville. He moved to Epic Records in 1979 and had more hits there, including "Down on the Rio Grande" and "Foolin'," before his chart success tapered off in the late 1980s.

Later years

Rodriguez struggled at times with personal troubles, and he was open about addiction. He kept touring for decades, playing in the United States and abroad, including shows at the Ryman Auditorium and Carnegie Hall. He played at President George H. W. Bush's inaugural ball, and he was inducted into the Texas Country Music Hall of Fame in 2007. In 2010 he received a pioneer award recognizing him as the first major Hispanic singer in country music.

Johnny Rodriguez died on 9 May 2025, in hospice care in San Antonio, at the age of 73.

What really happened

The goat might be legend, or at least an embellished version of a minor run-in with the law. The Ranger who heard him sing remembered an unpaid fine, not a barbecue. But the core of the story holds up across every account: a teenager in a small-town Texas jail sang, a Texas Ranger listened, a promoter with a movie-set town gave him a stage, and two Nashville stars told him to go for it. Goat or no goat, it is one of the more unlikely starts in country music history.
